## Precisely proved

At a fixed generic zero-avoiding rational Euler line, fix any nonzero complex compact smooth test g, one real Gevrey step phi0, and any compact interval inside its strict transition. For the full class of arbitrarily small real smooth perturbations supported in that interval, the following are equivalent:

- The actual reciprocal j has polynomial growth.
- One fixed compact-frequency vector q remains in the maximal inverse domain after every physical cutoff.
- Every actual alternate core commutator has a bounded extension.
- Every such commutator has a trace-class extension.

The test and the vectors q and w are fixed before varying the cutoff. The cutoff range 0≤phi≤1 is retained. The theorem does not impose monotonicity or reflection symmetry.

Polynomial growth of j, together with the already proved polynomial derivatives of A, gives polynomial derivatives of j. The original sequential composition is then defined on the whole fixed core for every compact smooth test/cutoff, its divided-difference kernel is Schwartz, and the actual finite-frequency operators converge in trace norm.

Conversely, boundedness is tested against one fixed output w. The adjoint pairing forces j T_phi q into L2. A Baire argument makes the universal cutoff-domain map continuous; localized modulations then bound local L2 norms of j by a polynomial. The generic polynomial logarithmic derivative turns those bounds into pointwise polynomial growth.

If polynomial growth fails, the good-domain perturbations form a meagre F_sigma in the real compact-support Frechet space. A bad perturbation can be selected arbitrarily close to zero and inside the permitted cutoff range. The smooth function H_phi in the exact output pairing is not L2. Normalizing compact cutoffs of H_phi gives actual unit core vectors with divergent output norms. The finite RS007 operators converge on each core vector to this unbounded core operator, so their norms cannot stay bounded. Their scalar traces still converge by the existing logarithmic-derivative theorem.
