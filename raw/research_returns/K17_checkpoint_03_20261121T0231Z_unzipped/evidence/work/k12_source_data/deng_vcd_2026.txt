                                                   The proof relies on Franke’s filtration of the weighted automorphic cochain complex, developed in
                                                   his proof of Borel’s conjecture. We treat trivial and nontrivial cuspidal support separately. For
                                                   nontrivial support, we combine a degree calculation with the Chenevier–Lannes lower bound of

Borel–Serre [1] prove that Nn is the virtual cohomological dimension of Γn . Its rational cohomology
vanishes above Nn . We ask how far this vanishing extends below Nn .
  The spherical Tits building Bn of Qn is the simplicial complex of flags of nonzero proper Q-
subspaces of Qn [2, Section 2]. Its top reduced homology is the rational Steinberg module
                                          Stn = H
                                                e n−2 (Bn ; Q).
Borel–Serre virtual duality [1, Theorem 11.4.2] gives
                                   H Nn −i (Γn ; Q) ∼
                                                    = Hi (Γn ; Stn ).
Church, Farb, and Putman [2, Conjecture 2] conjectured that these groups vanish for 0 ≤ i < n − 1.
  For the automorphic argument we express this question in terms of compact supports. Set
                        Xn = SLn (R)/ SOn ,        dn = dim Xn = Nn + n − 1.
Poincaré–Lefschetz duality identifies the dual of H Nn −i (Γn ; C) with compactly supported cohomology
in degree n − 1 + i. The conjecture is therefore equivalent to
                                 Hcj (Γn \Xn ; C) = 0      (j < 2n − 2).
Here the part with j < n − 1 corresponds to the already known vanishing above Nn .
1.2. Main result and previous work.
Theorem 1.1. For every n ≥ 2 and every 0 ≤ i < n − 1,
(1.1)                                      H Nn −i (Γn ; Q) = 0.
  Lee–Szczarba [3, Theorem 4.1] proved the top degree in the conjecture, Church–Putman [4]
proved the codimension-one case, and Brück–Miller–Patzt–Sroka–Wilson [5, Theorem B] proved the
codimension-two case. These arguments use the Steinberg-module formulation of Borel–Serre duality.
See [6] for a most recent account of the conjecture. In this work, we take a different approach by
considering the automorphic description of cohomology, in which the full power of Franke’s work [7]
will be employed. The proof, given in Subsection 9.2, treats the entire range 0 ≤ i < n − 1 by the
same argument.
1.3. The automorphic framework. Franke’s comparison theorem computes ordinary arithmetic
cohomology from a relative Lie algebra complex of automorphic forms [7, Theorem 18]. After fixing a
growth bound, cuspidal support, and infinitesimal-character ideal, the resulting weighted subcomplex
carries a finite Franke filtration, defined by constant-term exponents. Its associated graded pieces
are described by Laurent coefficients of Eisenstein series [7, Theorem 14 and Corollary 1]. Thus
the first task is to determine which inducing data can appear in those graded pieces. The precise
comparison maps and grading conventions used below are fixed in Subsection 2.7.
   Here inducing data consist of a parabolic subgroup R = MR NR , a square-integrable automorphic
representation σ of its Levi subgroup MR , and a spectral parameter λ specifying the twist in
normalized parabolic induction. Only data for which the induced representation has the infinitesimal
character of the trivial representation can contribute to the cohomology groups H ∗ (Γn ; C). This
imposes a condition on the possible infinitesimal characters of the representation of MR which we
explain in the next paragraph.
   By Mœglin–Waldspurger’s classification of the residual spectrum of GLn [8], the relevant square-
integrable Levi representations are products of Speh representations. A Speh representation J(ℓ, π)
is determined by a cuspidal representation π and an integer ℓ > 0. Its Harish–Chandra parameter
is the unordered multiset representing its infinitesimal character; see Definition 3.1. Up to a
common translation, this parameter consists of a string of ℓ consecutive entries for each entry of the
Harish–Chandra parameter of π. The trivial cuspidal character π = 1 gives
                                          J(m, 1) = 1GLm (A) .

After including the inducing twists, we normalize the full parameter to have coordinate sum zero and
shift it by (n − 1)/2, as in Subsection 3.1. For the ν-th inducing factor, let Bν be the set of entries it
contributes to this normalized and shifted parameter. The infinitesimal-character condition becomes
a tiling condition: the sets Bν must be pairwise disjoint and have union Un = {0, 1, . . . , n − 1}.
   The same tiling controls the possible degrees. If q is the ordinary automorphic cohomological
degree, set j = dn − q, which corresponds to the degree of the Poincaré–Lefschetz dual. In the
degree range under consideration, q > dn − (2n − 2), hence j < 2n − 2. Proposition 3.2 decomposes
j into three pieces according to the inducing data: the cohomology of the individual Levi factors,
the roots joining distinct factors, and the relative split centre. Reflection of the subsets Bν gives the
pairwise lower bound (3.8). Apart from the calculation for the trivial support in Theorem 3.4, the
only arithmetic input needed for these estimates is the Chenevier–Lannes consequence recorded in
Lemma 3.3:
                                               w(π) ≥ 11
for level-one regular algebraic cuspidal representations of rank at least two.
   In the nontrivial support case, the next subsection describes the resulting reduction to a maximal
parabolic and the construction of a primitive in the full automorphic complex.
1.4. Reduction to maximal parabolic inducing data. For nontrivial support, the proof has
three parts. Fix j < 2n − 2.
   1. Reduce to a maximal parabolic. Proposition 7.3 shows that, in the range j < 2n − 2, only
inducing data with square-integrable Levi representation
                      J(m, 1) ⊠ J(ℓ, π),      k = rank(π) ≥ 2,        n = m + kℓ,
can contribute to the cohomology of the filtration quotients. The subset contributed by J(m, 1) is
an interval of length m in the interior of Un ; the subset contributed by J(ℓ, π) fills its complement.
Lemma 8.1 shows that k is even and that exactly k/2 of the length-ℓ segments contributed by J(ℓ, π)
lie on each side of this interval. The roots joining the two factors therefore contribute mkℓ/2 to the
degree j as determined by Proposition 3.2.
   2. Choose a regular cut. Proposition 6.6 reduces the argument to closed tensor summands
c = α∨ ⊗ cS , with dS cS = 0. Here α∨ is an invariant Levi cochain dual to a compact-dual monomial
α ∈ H ∗ (SUm / SOm ; Q). We seek an ordered decomposition m = a + b, with a, b ≥ 1, whose
associated cut has nonzero restriction of α and for which the Eisenstein family satisfies the regularity
conditions of Definition 5.5.
   Let d(α) count the cuts with ι∗a,b α ̸= 0, where ιa,b is the compact-dual map of Section 5. Lemma 5.4
gives
                                          deg α + 2d(α) ≥ 2m − 2.
Thus low degree forces many cuts to have nonzero restriction. At most ℓ cuts are nonregular, even
when a central L-value vanishes (Lemma 8.4). For each such summand representing a nonzero
cohomology class, Proposition 8.6 combines these bounds with the degree formula and w(π) ≥ 11 to
produce a regular cut with ι∗a,b α ̸= 0 whenever j < 2n − 2.
   3. Construct a primitive. Apply the Eisenstein family for J(m, 1) ⊠ J(ℓ, π) to the induced cochains
associated with c. The prescribed values and derivatives in the spectral parameter give a closed
automorphic cochain (Em )D (c), as in Definition 6.8.
   For the chosen ordered decomposition m = a + b, with regular associated cut, we use an induced
family with factors of ranks a and b. After normalization, the residue of the resulting Eisenstein
family is the original family applied to the inducing section regarded as an element of the J(m, 1)-
factor ; see (5.18). Holomorphic dependence on the remaining spectral parameters allows us to
differentiate this identity. The residue calculation uses Mœglin–Waldspurger’s description of the
residual spectrum [8, Section II.3] and detection of poles by cuspidal constant terms, as recalled by
Bernstein–Lapid [9, Equation (6.18) and Section 8.6].

  Proposition 6.11 combines the residue identity (5.18) with the homotopy on finite distributions of
Lemma 4.4 to construct a cochain η one degree lower satisfying
                                          daut η = (Em )D (c).
    Finally, consider an arbitrary cocycle of degree q with nontrivial support and complementary
degree j = dn − q < 2n − 2. Choose a growth bound that places it in a complex with a finite
filtration. At each filtration level, Lemma 9.1 uses the primitives above to subtract a boundary in
the full automorphic complex, leaving a cocycle in the next filtration term. Since the filtration is
finite, this process ends at zero. The original cocycle is therefore a boundary. Together with the
trivial-support vanishing and Franke’s comparison, this proves Theorem 1.1.
    We illustrate the argument with a rank 12 example in Section 9.3.

1.5. Organization of the paper. Section 2 fixes the automorphic and cohomological conventions.
Sections 3 and 4 give the degree estimates and the distributional identity. Section 5 establishes the
compact-dual restriction formulas and the Eisenstein residue identity at a regular cut. Section 6 uses
them to construct an automorphic boundary. Section 7 reduces the possible low-degree contributions
to inducing data on maximal parabolics. Section 8 supplies a regular cut for each remaining class.
Section 9 assembles the argument.

1.6. Scope and integral questions. The theorem concerns full level and trivial rational coefficients.
Full level is used both in the spherical intertwiner calculation and in (3.9). Trivial coefficients give
the consecutive weight set Un and the compact-dual calculation for J(m, 1). The top-degree rational
cohomology of principal congruence subgroups of prime level has nonzero finite-field Steinberg
quotients [10, Section 1]. Nontrivial algebraic coefficients would also require different weight bounds
and local intertwiner estimates. For SL4 (Z) and GL4 (Z), Bajpai–Deng [11] compute homological
Euler characteristics with irreducible rational algebraic coefficients and derive vanishing results and
lower bounds for the dimensions of cohomology groups.
   Let
                                          StZn = H
                                                 e n−2 (Bn ; Z).
The integral strengthening
                              Hi (SLn (Z); StZn ) = 0    (0 ≤ i < n − 1)
does not follow from our rational argument, which does not control torsion in integral Steinberg
homology. For integral results and conjectures, see [12, Sections 1.6–1.9].
   Integral Steinberg homology also enters the rank filtration in algebraic K-theory. Rognes’s
component-filtration spectral sequence computes the homology of the rank-filtration layers from
group homology with coefficients built from Steinberg modules [13, Theorems 7.8–7.9]. For related
iterated bar constructions, see [14, Sections 1.1–1.3]. The rank spectral sequence converges to
spectrum homology H∗ (K(Z); Z) [13, (8.3)]. Extracting K-groups by this approach requires resolving
differentials and extensions and further input relating spectrum homology to homotopy.
