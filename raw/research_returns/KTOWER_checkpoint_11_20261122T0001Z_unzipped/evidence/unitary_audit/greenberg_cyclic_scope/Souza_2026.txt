                                                                                1. Introduction
                                              The purpose of this expository note is to show how some of the results in the
                                           Iwasawa theory of Zp -extensions of number fields can be interpreted in the language
                                           of Sylvester rank functions.
                                              In the mid 1950’s, Kenkichi Iwasawa was interested in the following question:
                                           given a tower F = F0 ⊂ F1 ⊂ F2 ⊂ · · · of number fields, how does the size of the
                                           class group Cl(Fi ) grow? Recall that the ideal class group Cl(K) of a number field
                                           K with ring of integers OK is defined as the quotient JK /PK , where JK is the
                                           group of fractional ideals of OK and PK is the subgroup of principal ideals. This is
                                           always a finite group, and it measures precisely the failure of unique factorization
                                           in OK .
                                              In 1956, he gave a talk titled “A theorem on Abelian groups and its application in
                                           algebraic number theory.” on the Summer Meeting of the American Mathematical
                                           Society in Seattle. In his talk, he annouced the following remarkable theorem:
                                           Theorem 1.1 ([Iwa59]). Let F be a number field, and F = F0 ⊂      S F 1 ⊂ F2 ⊂ · · ·
                                           be a tower of finite normal extensions of F such that for L = i≥0 Fi we have
                                           Gal(L/F ) ≃ Zp . For each n, let Ln be the subfield of L fixed by pn Zp and pen be
                                           the highest power of p dividing the order of the class group Cl(Ln ). Then, there
                                           exist integers ν, λ ≥ 0 and µ ≥ 0 such that
                                                                                en = µpn + λn + ν
                                           for sufficiently large n.

    Our goal is to give a proof of Theorem 1.1 without resorting to the classification
of Zp [[G]]-modules up to pseudo-isomorphism. The way to go about this is to study
three natural families of rank functions on ring Zp [[G]] and to realize the quantities
appearing in Theorem 1.1 as the “rescaled sides” of a triangle formed by points in
these three families (to made clear in Section 3). These families all accumulate in
a common, canonical rank function on Zp [[G]], and computing the precise rate of
convergence for this accumulation yields Iwasawa’s formula.
    The structure of this note is as follows: we collect in Section 2 the definition
of Sylvester rank functions and some basic examples, the facts about the Iwawasa
algebra Zp [[G]] that will be used as well as the input from Global Class Field Theory
that allows us to deduce information about the ideal class groups along the tower
L/F . In Section 3, we define the three families of Sylvester rank functions on Zp [[G]]
which capture the quantities in Theorem 1.1, and we prove this precise relation in
Corollary 3.8. We then show how this Corollary immediately yields Theorem 1.1 for
the case where F contains a single prime over p, totally ramified in L, in Section 4.
The adaptations requried to treat the general case are presented in Section 5.

   If R is a field and rk is the usual notion of rank from linear algebra (i.e. number
of pivots in a row-echelon form), then axioms (SMat1) through (SMat4) hold. In
fact, in this case, rk is the unique Sylvester matrix rank function defined on R: it
follows from (SMat2) that rk(U ) = n for any invertible matrix U ∈ Matn (R), and
again that rk(U A) = rk(AV ) = rk(A) for any matrix A and any pair (U, V ) of
invertible matrices of the appropriate sizes. Thus, for an arbitrary rank function
rk, rk(A) is determined by the rank of any row-echelon form. This must in turn
coincide with the number of pivots by (SMat1) and (SMat3).

2.3. Global Class Field Theory. Let K be a number field, that is, a finite
extension of Q, and denote by OK its ring of integers. A fractional ideal of K is a
subset I ⊂ K of the form r−1 J, where J is an ideal of OK . It is invertible if there
exists another fractional ideal J ⊂ K such that IJ = OK . The set of all invertible
fractional ideals forms an abelian group JK under multiplication, and it contains
the subgroup PK of all principal fractional ideals OK x for non-zero x ∈ K. The
quotient Cl(K) = JK /PK forms an abelian group called the ideal class group of
K. The ring OK is a principal ideal domain if and only if Cl(K) = 1. The class
number of K is the order h(K) = |Cl(K)| of its ideal class group, and is a measure
of how much does unique factorization fails in the ring OK .

Gal(L/K). Recall the existence of a multiplicative Galois norm map NL/K : L → K
