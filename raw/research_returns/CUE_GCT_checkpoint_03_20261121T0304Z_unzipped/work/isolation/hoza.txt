                                                    Abstract
          We present an explicit weight assignment generator that uses a seed of length O(log(wn)) to
      assign a polynomially-bounded integer weight to each vertex of any simple width-w length-n
      layered digraph. For each such digraph, there is at least one seed such that the weight function
      is min-isolating, i.e., the minimum-weight path from s to t is unique for every s, t such that t is
      reachable from s. The generator is computable using O(log(wn)) bits of space. Thanks to work
      by Reinhardt and Allender (SICOMP 2000), it immediately follows that NL = UL, i.e., every
      nondeterministic log-space Turing machine can be simulated by an unambiguous log-space Turing
      machine. The construction and analysis of the min-isolating weight function are remarkably
      simple.


1     Introduction
We say that a nondeterministic Turing machine is unambiguous if, for every input, the number
of accepting computation paths is either zero or one. Let UL be the set of languages that can be
decided by unambiguous log-space Turing machines. Trivially, UL ⊆ NL. It has been commonly
conjectured that the two complexity classes are equal. In this paper, we unconditionally prove that
the conjecture is correct.

Theorem 1.1. NL = UL.

1.1   Summary of prior work
Let us attempt to briefly summarize the large body of prior work on the NL vs. UL problem. The
class UL was introduced by Buntrock, Jenner, Lange, and Rossmanith [BJLR91] and Àlvarez and
Jenner [AJ93]. Arguably, the first indication that we should expect
                                                              ⨁︁ something like NL = UL to hold
came from the work of Gál and Wigderson, who proved NL ⊆        L/poly [Wig94; GW96]. Reinhardt
and Allender improved the containment to NL ⊆ UL/poly⨁︁using a beautiful inductive counting
argument [RA00]. (The class UL is trivially contained in       L.) Almost all subsequent work on
the NL vs. UL problem, including the present paper, builds on the techniques in Reinhardt and
Allender’s seminal paper [RA00].
    Allender, Reinhardt, and Zhou subsequently proved NL = UL assuming plausible circuit lower
bounds [ARZ99]; Li, Pyne, and Tell proved it under a milder hardness assumption [LPT24].
Unconditionally, Van Melkebeek and Prakriya proved that every language in NL can be decided
by an unambiguous Turing machine using O(log3/2 n) bits of space and polynomial time [vMP19];
Kallampally and Tewari independently achieved space O(log2 n) and polynomial time [KT16]. The
proof of Theorem 1.1 uses some of the ideas from Van Melkebeek and Prakriya’s work [vMP19].

1.2       Proof method: Min-isolating weight functions
The proof of Theorem 1.1 uses the notion of a min-isolating weight function.

Definition 1.2 (Min-isolating weight function). Let G = (V, E) be a digraph and let ρ : V → N.1
We consider the weight of a path with respect to ρ to be the sum of the weights of the internal
vertices of the path. We say that ρ is min-isolating for G if, for every pair of vertices u, v ∈ V such
that v is reachable from u, there is a unique path from u to v with minimum weight with respect to
ρ.

   The generic Isolation Lemma of Mulmuley, Vazirani, and Vazirani [MVV87] implies that a
random polynomially-bounded weight function is min-isolating for G with high probability. The
key contribution of this paper is a derandomized isolation lemma for the specific case of isolating
minimum-weight paths in layered digraphs.

Theorem 1.3 (An explicit min-isolating weight assignment generator for layered digraphs). For
every w, n ∈ N, there exists an explicit “weight assignment generator”

                                       ρ : {0, 1}ℓ × ([w] × {0, 1, . . . , n}) → N

with the following properties.

       • For every simple width-w length-n layered digraph G,2 there exists a seed x ∈ {0, 1}ℓ such that
         the weight function ρx (v) := ρ(x, v) is min-isolating for G.

       • The seed length is ℓ = O(log(wn)), the output ρ(x, v) can be computed using O(log(wn)) bits
         of space given x and v, and the weights have magnitude ρ(x, v) ≤ poly(wn).

    The notion of a “weight assignment generator” was introduced√by Van Melkebeek and Prakriya,
who gave a construction
                     √     that uses a seed of length O(log(wn) · log n) and produces weights of
magnitude 2O(log(wn)· log n) [vMP19]. The notion is also implicit in earlier work, such as Reinhardt
and Allender’s proof that NL ⊆ UL/poly. The proof of Theorem 1.3 is remarkably simple.
    Given Theorem 1.3 and Reinhardt and Allender’s results [RA00], the conclusion NL = UL is
straightforward:

       • It is an exercise to show that reachability in layered digraphs is NL-complete.

    • Reinhardt and Allender designed unambiguous algorithms to check whether a given weight
      function ρ : V → N is min-isolating for a given digraph G = (V, E), as well as to decide
      reachability in G under the assumption that ρ is min-isolating [RA00]. Their algorithms use

    • Therefore, to decide reachability in layered digraphs, we can try all seeds x ∈ {0, 1}ℓ . For each
      seed, we use Reinhardt and Allender’s algorithm to check whether ρx is min-isolating for G
      [RA00]. If it is, we use ρx and Reinhardt and Allender’s other algorithm to decide reachability
      in G [RA00].
    The remainder of this paper consists of the proof of Theorem 1.3.


2     Min-isolating weights for layered digraphs
2.1    Construction
Let C : [w] → {0, 1}ℓ be an explicit error correcting code with minimum distance d. Eventually, we
will choose a large enough d = Θ(log(wn)). We can achieve such a minimum distance while also
ensuring that ℓ = O(log(wn)) and that C(a) can be computed using O(log(wn)) bits of space. For
example, we could use Justesen codes [Jus72].
    Let V be the vertex set, V = [w] × {0, 1, . . . , n}. For convenience, extend the error correcting
code C to the larger domain V by the rule C((a, i)) = C(a). For each seed x ∈ {0, 1}ℓ , we define a
weight function ρx : V → N by the following formula. If a vertex v is in layer i ∈ [n], then
                                               ℓ

                                   ρx (v) =         6ν2 (i)+ℓ−j · C(v)j · xj ,                          (1)

where ν2 (i) is the 2-adic valuation of i, i.e., ν2 (i) := max{r : 2r divides i}. (There is nothing special
about the number 6; any large enough constant would work in its place.) If v is in layer zero, then
ρx (v) = 0. Clearly, this generator can be computed in space O(log(wn)), and it outputs integer
weights that are at most poly(nw).
    The factor of 6ν2 (i) in Eq. (1) is reminiscent of Van Melkebeek and Prakriya’s constructions
[vMP19], which also use larger magnitude weights in layers where ν2 (i) is large.

2.2    Analysis
We require the following elementary fact about the 2-adic valuation function.
Lemma 2.1. Let I be a finite, nonempty, interval subset of Z>0 . Let M = maxi∈I ν2 (i). Then for
every ∆ ≥ 0, there are at most 2∆ values i ∈ I such that ν2 (i) = M − ∆. In particular, there is a
unique i∗ ∈ I such that ν2 (i∗ ) = M .
Proof. For ∆ = 0, uniqueness holds because between any two integers i, i′ with ν2 (i) = ν2 (i′ ), there
exists i′′ with ν2 (i′′ ) > ν2 (i). We prove the ∆ > 0 case by induction on M . The base case M = 0 is
trivial. For the inductive step, let IL be the set of elements of I less than i∗ , and let IR be the set
of elements of I greater than i∗ . By the uniqueness of i∗ , every i ∈ IL ∪ IR satisfies ν2 (i) ≤ M − 1.
Therefore, by induction, the number of i ∈ IL such that ν2 (i) = M − ∆ is at most 2∆−1 , and the
number of i ∈ IR such that ν2 (i) = M − ∆ is also at most 2∆−1 .
