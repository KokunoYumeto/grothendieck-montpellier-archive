                                             1. Introduction
   Let1 G be a (connected)2 reductive group3 over a field k. For example, G might be GLn
or Sp2n . The Steinberg representation of G encodes the combinatorial structure of the
parabolic4 k-subgroups of G and plays a fundamental role in representation theory and
algebraic topology. For the classical split groups
                                G ∈ {GLn , SLn , Sp2n , SOn,n , SOn,n+1 },
Ash–Putman–Sam [2] proved that the homology of G(k) with coefficients in the Steinberg
representation vanishes in a range. Using a new approach inspired by Miller–Patzt–Wilson’s
work on the Rognes connectivity conjecture [22], this paper extends [2] to all reductive
groups.5 Even for the classical split groups, our approach is technically easier.6
   Church–Farb–Putman [14] made a vanishing conjecture about the high-dimensional
cohomology of SLn (Z). By Borel–Serre duality, this is equivalent to the vanishing in a stable
range of the homology of SLn (Z) with coefficients in the Steinberg representation of SLn (Q).
This can be viewed as an integral refinement of the SLn case Ash–Putman–Sam’s vanishing
theorem.
   We show that our approach to the vanishing of the homology of SLn (Q) with coefficients
in its Steinberg representation can be extended to SLn (Z) if a certain simplicial complex
is highly connected, and prove this high connectivity in some simple cases. Using this, we
refine the known cases of this conjecture in degrees 1 and 2.

1.1. Tits building. Let the semisimple k-rank of G be n. The spherical Tits building for
G, denoted T (G), is an (n − 1)-dimensional simplicial complex whose r-simplices are the
proper parabolic k-subgroups P of G whose semisimple k-rank is n − 1 − r. The simplex
corresponding to a proper parabolic k-subgroup P′ is a face of the one corresponding to P
if7 P ⊂ P′ . The conjugation action of G(k) on itself permutes k-points P(k) of the different
parabolic k-subgroups P, giving an action of G(k) on T (G). See [34, 8] for more details.
Example 1.1. If G is either GLn+1 or SLn+1 , then the semisimple k-rank of G is n and the
parabolic k-subgroups of G are the stabilizers of flags
(1.1)                            0 ⊊ V0 ⊊ V1 ⊊ · · · ⊊ Vr ⊊ k n+1
of subspaces of k n+1 . The quotient of G by the stabilizer of (1.1) is a flag variety. The
semisimple k-rank of the stabilizer of (1.1) is n − 1 − r. The Tits building T (G) is the
simplicial complex whose r-simplices are length-r flags of subspaces of k n+1 .          □
Remark 1.2. If G has no proper parabolic k-subgroups, then its semisimple k-rank is 0 and
T (G) = ∅. Such G are called anisotropic. Easy examples include GL0 and GL1 . A more
interesting example is the orthogonal group of a quadratic form q on a k-vector space V
that is anisotropic, i.e., such that the only ⃗v ∈ V with q(⃗v ) = 0 is ⃗v = 0.         □
1.2. Steinberg representation. Let F be a commutative ring. The Solomon–Tits theorem
[28] says that T (G) is homotopy equivalent to a wedge of (n − 1)-dimensional spheres.
The Steinberg representation of G with coefficients in F, denoted St(G; F), is its reduced
(n − 1)-dimensional homology group with coefficients in F, i.e., St(G; F) = He n−1 (T (G); F).
For F = Z, we will omit F from our notation and just write St(G). The action of G(k) on
T (G) induces an action of G(k) on St(G; F). See [17, 32] for surveys.
   These are finite-dimensional representations of G(k) when k is finite, and Steinberg
and Curtis [29, 30, 31, 16] showed that if k is finite and F is a field, then St(G; F) is
usually8 an irreducible representation of G(k). Except in degenerate cases, for infinite k the
representation St(G; F) is infinite-dimensional. Putman–Snowden [24] showed that in this
case St(G; F) is irreducible for all fields F.
Remark 1.3. If G is anisotropic, then n = 0 and T (G) = ∅. Our convention is St(G; F) =

1.3. Weak form of main theorem. We can now state a weak form of our main theorem.
Theorem A (Weak form). Let G be a reductive group over a field k with semisimple
k-rank n and let F be a commutative ring. Then Hi (G(k); St(G; F)) = 0 for i ≤ b(n), where
b : N → Z is a function with limn7→∞ b(n) = ∞.
Remark 1.4. For anisotropic groups G, we have n = 0. The function b satisfies b(0) = −1,
so our theorem says nothing nontrivial about such G.                                  □
Remark 1.5. If k is a finite field and G(k) is a finite group of Lie type, then St(G; k) is
a projective G(k)-module (see [17]) and thus Hi (G(k); St(G; k)) = 0 for i ≥ 0. However,
St(G; F) is not projective for a general commutative ring like F = Z, so even for finite groups
of Lie type Theorem A has nontrivial content.                                                □

1.8. Connectivity of double Tits building. The complex T 2 (Zn ) is (2n − 3)-dimensional.
Miller–Patzt–Wilson [22] proved that if it was (2n − 4)-connected, then the Rognes connec-
tivity conjecture [26, Conjecture 12.3] would hold for the ring Z. Their results show that
this would also imply the following slight weakening of Conjecture 1.11:
      • For n ≥ 2 and F a field of characteristic 0, we have Hi (GLn (Z); St(GLn ; F)) =
        Hi (SLn (Z); St(SLn ; F)) = 0 for i ≤ ⌊(n − 2)/2⌋.
This combines results from [22] (cf. Theorem 23.3 below) with [19, Theorems 7.2 & 7.8].
Remark 1.13. By [2] (which is generalized in Theorem B), for all commutative rings F we
have Hi (GLn (Q); St(GLn ; F)) = Hi (SLn (Q); St(SLn ; F)) = 0 for i ≤ ⌊(n − 2)/2⌋. This is the
same range Miller–Patzt–Wilson’s work conjecturally gives for GLn (Z) and SLn (Z).           □
Remark 1.14. It is natural to hope that T 2 (Zn ) is (2n − 4)-connected. Indeed, if we
were working over a field k rather than Z, then it is easy to see that any two flags are

1.9. Integral theorems. We refine this. Roughly speaking, the following says that to
prove vanishing for GLn (Z) and SLn (Z) up to degree i = b, we must prove that T 2 (Zn ) is
(n − 2 + b)-connected for n ≫ 0:
Theorem C. Fix b ≥ 1. Assume that the following holds for all n ≥ 4:
(†)                 The space T 2 (Zn ) is n − 2 + min(b, ⌊(n − 1)/2⌋)-connected.
Then Hi (GLn (Z); St(GLn ; F)) = Hi (SLn (Z); St(SLn ; F)) = 0 for all commutative rings F and
all n, i ≥ 0 such that:
       • i ≤ min(b, ⌊(n − 2)/2⌋) if 2 and 3 are invertible in F; and
       • i ≤ min(b, ⌊(n − 3)/3⌋) in general.
Remark 1.15. As we will see, for n ≫ 0 the hypothesis (†) gives a length-b partial resolution of
St(GLn ; F) from which Hi (GLn (Z); St(GLn ; F)) can be inductively calculated, and similarly
for SLn (Z). Similar resolutions appeared in the proofs of the cases i = 1 and i = 2 of
Conjecture 1.11; however, it was unclear how to extend those resolutions further.             □
Remark 1.16. More generally, if T 2 (Zn ) is (n − 2 + b)-connected for n ≫ 0 then our proof
will show that Hi (GLn (Z); St(GLn ; F)) = Hi (SLn (Z); St(SLn ; F)) = 0 for i ≤ b and n ≫ 0.
We remark that T 2 (Zn ) will not be (n − 2 + b)-connected for small b, which is why our
connectivity assumption in Theorem C is phrased the way it is.

1.10. Outline. We start in Part 1 with some general results about reductive groups. This
part closes by reducing Theorem B to the special case where kΦ (G) is irreducible and
non-exceptional, i.e., kΦ (G) ∈ {An , Bn , Cn , BCn , Dn }. The heart of the paper consists of
Parts 2 and 3 and 4, which prove Theorem B for those root systems. The three cases are
kΦ (G) = An , and kΦ (G) ∈ {Bn , Cn , BCn }, and kΦ (G) = Dn . In Part 5 we generalize this
to SLn (Z) and GLn (Z) and prove Theorem C. Finally, in Part 6 we prove Theorem D.
1.11. Conventions. Unless otherwise specified, all algebraic groups G are defined over
a field k that is fixed throughout the paper. All subgroups, morphisms, quotients, etc.
we discuss are closed and defined over k. In particular, we will just talk about parabolic
subgroups rather than parabolic k-subgroups and the semisimple rank of a group rather
than the semisimple k-rank. All reductive groups are connected.

Part 1. General theory (Theorem B)
   We survey some general properties of reductive groups in §2. Next, in §3 we perform
some initial reductions. The main tool for our proofs is a spectral sequence we introduce in
§4. To show how this spectral sequence can be used, in §5 we prove a surjectivity result in
rank 2. We then develop some tools for understanding reducible root systems in §6, and
close in §7 by reducing Theorem B to the case where the relative root system is irreducible
and non-exceptional. Those cases will be handled in subsequent parts.

30.6. Outline of rest of paper. The rest of this paper is devoted to proving Theorem D′ .

         ⟨ϵ1 vp1 + ϵ2 vp2 ⟩ to F ′ .
