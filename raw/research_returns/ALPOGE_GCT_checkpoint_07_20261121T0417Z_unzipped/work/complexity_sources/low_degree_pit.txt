                                                We prove unconditionally that either NE contains a language requiring Boolean circuits
                                            of size at least 2n /(2n), or VP0Z ̸= VNP0Z , a constant-free version of Valiant’s conjecture over the
                                            integers. The Boolean alternative implies that NP ̸⊆ SIZE[nk ] for every constant k ≥ 1. Our
                                            proof gives a fine-grained variant of the connection between Polynomial Identity Testing (PIT)
                                            and circuit lower bounds established by Kabanets and Impagliazzo (2004). We show that the
                                            specific identity tests required by this argument can be performed over the integers without
                                            a derandomization assumption.
                                                Over a fixed finite field F, we first give a deterministic O(std−1 )-time algorithm for PIT
                                            on size-s circuits computing degree-d polynomials in t variables. We further show that, for any
                                            fixed d ≥ 2 and ε > 0, improving this running time by a factor of tε , even nondeterministically,
                                            implies either the same Boolean lower bound or an arithmetic circuit lower bound of t1+ε/2
                                            for a polynomial family from VNPF . If nondeterministic PIT instead runs in time so(d) t(1−ε)d
                                            for infinitely many degrees d, then the arithmetic lower bound strengthens to VPF ̸= VNPF .
                                            Over the integers, we give a deterministic PIT algorithm for syntactically set-multilinear circuits
                                            running in time sO(1) t5d/6 , which yields the unconditional result above.
                                                Combining the Kabanets–Impagliazzo argument with range avoidance, we show that an NP
                                            algorithm for PIT over Z gives a purely arithmetic win–win: either VPZ ̸= VNPZ , or constant-
                                            degree polynomials of maximum circuit complexity can be constructed in FNP.
                                                Along the way, we show that arithmetic circuits of size s(N ) for the permanent, or for
                                            an explicit family in VNP, give perfectly complete input-oblivious Merlin–Arthur protocols
                                            running in time s(O(n)) nO(1) for 3-UNSAT with O(n) clauses; thus ruling out faster protocols
                                            yields arithmetic lower bounds that scale with the protocol’s running time.

1    Introduction
Proving circuit lower bounds is one of the central challenges of complexity theory. Such bounds
establish computational hardness directly: they show that every circuit solving a given problem
must be large. A boolean circuit is a computation built from simple boolean operations, such as
AND, OR, and NOT gates and its size is the number of gates. Every polynomial-time algorithm
can be simulated by polynomial-size circuits with very small overhead, so a superpolynomial circuit
lower bound for SAT would separate P from NP.
    Despite the simplicity of this model, our understanding of unrestricted circuits remains very
limited. For a function in P, the strongest known lower bound is 3.5n − o(n), due to Mishra
and Santhanam [MS26], improving the earlier bounds (3 + 1/86)n − o(n) [FGHK16] and 3.1n −
o(n) [LY22]. Moving to the much larger class ENP = DTIME[2O(n) ]NP , which allows exponential-time
computation with an NP oracle, did not help: until very recently, no superlinear lower bound was
known even there √ [GKST18]. This changed with a recent breakthrough of OpenAI [Ope26]: a
deterministic 2O(n/ log n) -time algorithm for 3-SAT on n variables with O(n) clauses, refuting the
Exponential Time Hypothesis. By known connections between satisfiability algorithms and circuit
lower bounds [IP01, Wil13, JMV18], this algorithm
                                              √     yields the first superlinear lower bound for ENP :
a language that requires circuits of size Ω(n log n) at infinitely many input
                                                                          √     lengths. Yet even an
algorithmic advance of this magnitude lifts the lower bound only by a log n factor above linear,
and the following modest improvement remains open.

Open Problem 1. Is there a language in ENP that requires Boolean circuits of size Ω(n log n)?

    The algebraic analogue of a Boolean circuit replaces Boolean gates by arithmetic operations.
An arithmetic circuit over a ring R computes from variables and constants of R using addition
and multiplication; each gate computes a polynomial, and the circuit computes the polynomial of
its output gate. Valiant’s classes VP and VNP formalize the distinction between efficient algebraic
computation and algebraic counting [Val79a, Val82]. The class VPR consists of families of polynomials
with polynomially many variables and polynomially bounded degree that have polynomial-size
arithmetic circuits over R. The class VNPR consists of the families {Pt } of the form

Open Problem 2. For a fixed finite field F, is there an explicit family of polynomials in t variables,
of degree polynomially bounded in t, whose arithmetic circuit complexity over F is ω(t log t)?

    Both targets are far less ambitious than the long-term goals of near-maximal Boolean hardness,
on the scale 2n /n, and exponential arithmetic hardness of the m × m permanent, on the scale 2Ω(m) .
Yet even they are beyond current techniques.
    Perhaps surprisingly, both questions are closely connected to a central problem in derandomiza-
tion. Polynomial identity testing (PIT) asks whether a given arithmetic circuit computes the zero
polynomial; testing whether two circuits compute the same polynomial reduces to this problem by
taking their difference. Randomness makes this task easy: evaluate the circuit at a random point
from a sufficiently large set, and a nonzero polynomial is unlikely to vanish. Over small finite fields
one can evaluate in an extension field, and over the integers one can compute modulo a random
prime to keep the numbers short [DL78, Zip79, Sch80]. Thus PIT has a randomized polynomial-time
algorithm, whereas the best known deterministic algorithms for general circuits take exponential
time.
    Kabanets and Impagliazzo [KI04] showed that derandomizing this single problem would already
imply major circuit lower bounds, even if the derandomized algorithm is nondeterministic and runs
in subexponential time. Together with subsequent refinements [KvMS12, AvM11], their argument
gives the following statement.

    This argument also verifies identities involving the permanent, whose degree grows with the
matrix dimension.2 How much of this identity-testing power is actually needed? In particular, could
faster algorithms for polynomials of constant degree already imply new lower bounds? Over a fixed
finite field, constant-degree PIT is already in deterministic polynomial time: computing, gate by

      Question 1. How weak a derandomization of PIT suffices to imply new circuit lower
      bounds? In particular, can a non-trivial speed-up for constant-degree polynomials, for
      which polynomial-time algorithms are already known, have such consequences?

    One can also ask whether the derandomization assumption can be removed altogether. Here the
precedents concern classes whose verifiers may use randomness. If the permanent has polynomial-size
arithmetic circuits over Z, then Merlin can send such circuits and Arthur can check them using
the downward self-reducibility of the permanent and randomized identity tests [KI04]; this places
P#P , and hence ΣP2 , in MA. Combined with Kannan’s theorem [Kan82], this gives a win–win: either
PERM ∈   / VPZ , or MA ̸⊆ SIZE[nk ] for every constant k. Santhanam [San09] removed the arithmetic
alternative at the cost of one bit of advice, proving that MA/1 ̸⊆ SIZE[nk ] for every constant k.
At exponential time, Kabanets and Impagliazzo [KI04] also proved, unconditionally, that either
NEXPRP ̸⊆ P/poly or the permanent requires superpolynomial-size arithmetic circuits over Z; again,
the Boolean alternative concerns a class with randomness.
    More generally, fixed-polynomial lower bounds are known for ΣP2 [Kan82], for symmetric polyno-
mial time SP2 [Cai07, CHLR26] and its oblivious counterpart [GLV24], and for PP [Vin05, Aar06];
moreover, symmetric exponential time requires near-maximum circuit size [CHLR26]. Each of these
classes uses a second quantifier, randomness, or counting. For NP and NE, whose verifiers are
deterministic and use a single quantifier, no such bounds are known.
    This leaves a sharper goal: remove the identity-testing assumption from the Kabanets–
Impagliazzo theorem while keeping the Boolean lower bound for a class with a deterministic
verifier.

      Question 2. Can we prove an unconditional Boolean–arithmetic win–win in which the
      Boolean lower bound holds for NE, without randomized verification or advice?

   We answer both questions. For Question 1, we show that, over a fixed finite field, shaving a
polynomial factor off the best known deterministic running time for constant-degree PIT, even with
a nondeterministic algorithm, would resolve Open Problem 1 or Open Problem 2. For Question 2,
we show that over the integers the identity tests needed by our argument can be performed
unconditionally, which gives the main theorem below.

over Z is constant-free if the only constants at its leaves are 1 and −1. Let VP0Z be the class

1.1.1    The Route to the Main Theorem: Constant-Degree Identity Testing
To prove Theorem 1, we first answer Question 1. We determine how fast an algorithm for constant-
degree identity testing must be before it implies new lower bounds. We then show that, over the
integers, the required speed-up can be achieved unconditionally for the identities that arise in our
proof. We consider the following promise problem.

  Problem: Promise f (t)-PITR

  Input: An arithmetic circuit C over a ring R of size s with t input variables, promised
  to compute a polynomial of degree at most f (t).

  Output:       Yes, if C computes the zero polynomial.
   3
     Every leaf, including a constant, has formal degree one; a sum gate has the maximum of the formal degrees of its
children, and a product gate has their sum. Bounding the formal degree also bounds the bit lengths of the coefficients
computed at all gates.




                                                          5
Here, the size of a circuit is the number of its gates together with the bit-sizes of all constants used
by the circuit. The promise concerns only the output polynomial; intermediate gates may compute
polynomials of larger degree. We write d-PITR when the degree bound is a constant d, and PITR
for the same problem without a promise on the degree. In all statements over a finite field F, the
field is fixed, and the constants hidden in the O(·) notation may depend on it.

A simple improvement. Over a fixed finite field, computing, gate by gate, the coefficients of all
monomials of degree at most d solves d-PITF in time Od (std ), as noted in the introduction. Our
first result saves a factor of t. The algorithm evaluates the circuit only at points with at most d − 1
nonzero coordinates. The only nonzero polynomials of degree at most d that vanish at all these
points are homogeneous multilinear of degree d, and such polynomials are detected through their
first-order partial derivatives. The construction of Baur and Strassen [BS83] computes all these
derivatives simultaneously, with only a constant-factor increase in circuit size (Section 3).
Theorem 2. Let F be a fixed finite field, and let d ≥ 1 be a constant. Then d-PITF on circuits of
size s with t variables can be solved deterministically in time Od (std−1 ).
    Over Z, there is no analogous baseline. Even a circuit without variables can compute an integer
with exponentially many bits, and testing whether it computes zero is polynomial-time equivalent
to general PITZ [ABKM09]. For this reason, our results on constant-degree identity testing are
stated over fixed finite fields.

Slightly faster algorithms imply strong lower bounds. Theorem 2 is a simple improvement,
so it is natural to ask how much further one can go. We show that any further polynomial saving,
even by a nondeterministic algorithm, would imply circuit lower bounds beyond current techniques.
Theorem 3. Let F be a finite field. Suppose that d-PITF ∈ NTIME[sa tb ] for a fixed integer d and
constants a ≥ 1 and b ≥ 0 satisfying a + b < d. Then at least one of the following holds:
  1. NE ̸⊆ SIZE[2n /(2n)];

  2. There is an explicit family {PN } of polynomials in O(N ) variables, depending only on the
     characteristic of F, such that, for every 1 < κ < (d − b)/a,

O(std−1−ε ) for some d ≥ 2 and ε ∈ (0, 1), then either NE ̸⊆ SIZE[2n /(2n)], or asizeF (PN ) > N 1+δ for

    Our proofs connect faster identity testing to faster nondeterministic algorithms for Boolean
circuit satisfiability. Assuming that U has small circuits, we use the hypothesized PIT algorithm
to decide unsatisfiability of Boolean circuits of size 2ρn in nondeterministic time O(2(1−ρ)n ), for

tables derandomize them, which gives NE ̸⊆ SIZE[2n /(2n)] (Section 7).

Closing the loop over the integers. To prove Theorem 1, we obtain the speed-up required
by Corollary 4 unconditionally, over the integers, for the identities that arise in our proof. These
identities are set-multilinear. Let the variables be split into disjoint blocks X1 , . . . , Xd of at most m
variables each. A polynomial is set-multilinear of type B ⊆ [d] if each of its monomials is the product
of exactly one variable from every block Xj with j ∈ B. A circuit is syntactically set-multilinear
if its gates α can be assigned types B(α) ⊆ [d] such that a leaf labeled by a variable of Xj has
type {j}, a leaf labeled by a nonzero integer has type ∅, a leaf labeled by zero has any type, both
children of a sum gate have its type, and the children of a product gate have disjoint types whose
union is its type. Then every gate α computes a set-multilinear polynomial fα of type B(α). For
a polynomial f over Z, let ∥f ∥1 be the sum of the absolute values of its coefficients, and let fΦ
be the polynomial computed by a circuit Φ. A polynomial of type [d] has up to md coefficients;
we test whether it is zero while saving a factor of m⌈d/6⌉ over computing all of them.

Theorem 5. Let d ≥ 2, and let the variables be split into d blocks of at most m variables each.
There is a deterministic algorithm that, given an integer L ≥ 1 and a syntactically set-multilinear
circuit Φ over Z with s gates in these variables whose output gate has type [d], behaves as follows.

   • If fΦ ̸= 0, then it rejects.

   • If fΦ = 0 and every gate α of Φ satisfies ∥fα ∥1 ≤ 2L , then it accepts.

   • If fΦ = 0 but some gate violates this bound, then it may accept or reject.

On every input, it runs in time md−⌈d/6⌉ · (dsL log(2m))O(1) .

    The algorithm uses the fact that, over the integers, a polynomial is zero if and only if the sum of the
squares of its coefficients is zero. It computes this sum without expanding the polynomial. Its running
time has the form sO(1) t(1−ε)d with ε = 1/6, which is exactly the regime of Corollary 4. To prove
Theorem 1, we run the argument behind Corollary 4 over Z with two changes. First, we replace U
by a partition polynomial in VNP0Z that yields every polynomial we need after substituting only the
constants 0 and 1 and variables. The assumption VP0Z = VNP0Z then gives constant-free circuits
of polynomial formal degree for all of them, so every gate computes a polynomial whose coefficients
have polynomial bit length, as Theorem 5 requires. Second, we replace the hypothesized PIT
algorithm by Theorem 5, applied to the set-multilinear part of each identity. Theorem 5 may
be of independent interest. We note that Raz and Shpilka [RS05] give a deterministic polynomial-
time algorithm for testing pure set-multilinear circuits, in which all product gates split types along
a single tree, but the circuits arising in our proof are not pure.

1.1.2    Further Applications
Fast oblivious Merlin–Arthur protocols from small circuits. Our second application
concerns input-oblivious Merlin–Arthur protocols [FSW09, GM15]. In such a protocol, Merlin’s
message depends only on the input length. For every input length, a single honest message must
make Arthur accept every yes-instance of this length, while on every no-instance, every message
is rejected with probability at least 2/3. The protocol is perfectly complete if the honest message
makes Arthur accept with probability one. The argument of Kabanets and Impagliazzo [KI04]
shows that small arithmetic circuits for the permanent give such protocols for coNP: Merlin sends
the circuits, and Arthur checks them with the downward self-reduction of the permanent. Combined
with the reduction of Ben-Dor and Halevi [BH93], these protocols lose only a constant factor in the
number of variables. We show that the same holds for the universal polynomial U over every finite
field, and for a language in ⊕P that computes the bits of U.4 Here, L⊕ ∈ ⊕P ∩ DTIME[2O(M/ log M ) ]
is a fixed language whose strings of length MN = Θ(N log N ) encode a point and a coordinate
of a value of UN over F2O(log N ) (Section 5).

Theorem 6. Let c ≥ 1 be an integer, let F be a finite field, and let U be the universal family
constructed for the characteristic of F. Let s : N → N be a nondecreasing function whose value can
be computed from N in time polynomial in N , and such that s(N ) ≤ 2N for all sufficiently large N .
Suppose that one of the following conditions holds for all sufficiently large N :

   1. asizeZ (PERMN ) ≤ s(N );

   2. asizeF (UN ) ≤ s(N );

   3. L⊕ has Boolean circuits with at most s(N ) gates on inputs of length MN .

Then there is a constant D ≥ 1, depending only on c and F, such that 3-UNSAT on n variables with
at most cn clauses has a perfectly complete input-oblivious Merlin–Arthur protocol running in time
s(Dn)nD .

Derandomization as a route to lower bounds. The algorithmic method of Williams [Wil13,
Wil14] shows that beating exhaustive search, or perebor [Tra84], even slightly, implies circuit lower
bounds. In particular, refuting the Exponential Time Hypothesis (ETH), the Strong Exponential
Time Hypothesis (SETH), or their nondeterministic variants would yield circuit lower bounds beyond
the reach of current techniques [Wil13, JMV18, MW18, CGI+ 16]. This implication has often been
read in reverse. Since we do not know how to prove such lower bounds, the corresponding algorithms
were taken to be out of reach, and the implication served as a barrier: the lower bound, it seemed,
would have to come before the algorithm.
    A recent wave of algorithmic breakthroughs invites us to revisit this belief. Algorithms for sparse
3-SAT found independently by OpenAI [Ope26] and by Anthropic [Ant26] refute the Exponential
Time Hypothesis. An algorithm discovered by Anthropic and developed by Alman and Vassilevska
Williams [AW26] refutes the 3SUM and APSP hypotheses of fine-grained complexity. The algorithm
of [Ope26, Ant26] already implies a superlinear lower bound for ENP , beyond the reach of all
previous techniques. Thus, the lower bound need not come first: it can come as a consequence
of the algorithm.
    The same belief has shaped the study of derandomization. Polynomial identity testing has seen
substantial progress for restricted models of computation (see Section 1.2). For general circuits,
however, the Kabanets–Impagliazzo theorem [KI04] was read as a warning. An efficient deterministic
identity test would prove lower bounds beyond current techniques, so it seemed out of reach, and
focusing on restricted models was the natural response.
    Our results make the case for targeting general circuits directly. Theorem 1 establishes uncon-
ditionally a form of the Kabanets–Impagliazzo conclusion. We obtain it the way the algorithmic
method obtains lower bounds: by designing a polynomial identity test that beats brute force

                                                     10
on exactly the instances our argument needs. These instances are structured integer circuits, and
the natural next step is to achieve such speed-ups for general constant-degree circuits over a finite
field. Shaving any polynomial factor off Theorem 2, even nondeterministically, would resolve Open
Problem 1 or Open Problem 2 (Theorem 3). Saving a constant fraction of the exponent of t, for
infinitely many degrees, would separate VPF from VNPF (Corollary 4), which in turn implies the
arithmetic alternative of Theorem 1.

1.2    Related Work
Unconditional circuit lower bounds. The class S2 P of symmetric polynomial time contains both
PNP and MA, and is contained in Σ2 P ∩ Π2 P. Its fixed-polynomial lower bounds [Cai07, CHLR26],
and those for its oblivious counterpart O2 P [GLV24], therefore strengthen Kannan’s lower bounds
for Σ2 P [Kan82]. Fixed-polynomial lower bounds for NP would be substantial progress even within
this line of work. Theorem 1 shows that either they hold, or the constant-free version of Valiant’s
conjecture holds. In the arithmetic setting, Fournier, Perifel, and de Joannis de Verclos [FPdJdV15]
showed, under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis, that for every k there is a family of polynomials
with coefficients in MA that requires arithmetic circuits of size larger than O(nk ) over C.

testing for constant-free circuits whose degree is bounded by their size, obtaining stronger trade-

average-case identity testing and average-case lower bounds. Andrews, Kush, and Tell [AKT25]
obtained polynomial-time identity testing over characteristic zero from nearly necessary lower-bound
assumptions in a model of uniform arithmetic networks. Bürgisser [Bür00] showed that, over
a field of positive characteristic, VP = VNP implies P/poly = NP/poly = PH/poly. In characteristic
zero, the same implication is known only under the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis. Beyond
hardness versus randomness, PIT is a central verification primitive in proof complexity. For example,
the Ideal Proof System of Grochow and Pitassi gives another route from proof lower bounds
to VP = ̸ VNP [GP18]. Dutta, Saxena, and Thierauf [DST24] showed that lower bounds for highly

1.3    Proof Overview
All our results follow the approach of Kabanets and Impagliazzo [KI04] described above: we guess
arithmetic circuits for a family of polynomials, verify them using a downward self-reduction, and
then use the verified circuits to solve a hard problem. For the permanent, this approach needs
identity tests of degree n for checking matrices of size n × n. Hence, it gives nothing for identity
testing of low-degree polynomials. Our approach replaces the permanent by polynomials that encode
satisfiability, whose degree we can choose, and which are still downward self-reducible. Moreover,
all these polynomials are projections of a single explicit family U ∈ VNPZ , so the assumption
that U has small circuits gives small circuits for all of them. The initial idea of encoding SAT
by low-degree polynomials was inspired by the polynomial formulations introduced in [BGK+ 25]
and used in [BKM+ 24] as a tool for studying barriers to proving fine-grained lower bounds for
various problems. Below, we describe the main ideas in greater detail.

Partition polynomials. The polynomials that replace the permanent in our proofs are called
partition polynomials. Recall that the permanent sums, over all permutations, the products of the
matrix entries selected by the permutation, one entry in each row. Similarly, a partition polynomial
sums, over all assignments to a set of Boolean variables, the products of the table entries selected
by the assignment, one entry in each table. Here, a table should be thought of as a constraint that
sees only some of the variables and has an entry for every assignment to them. Formally, for a list
S = (S1 , . . . , Sd ) of subsets of [r], table j ∈ [d] depends on the variables in Sj and has an entry wj,τ

2.1    Arithmetic Circuits and Identity Testing
An arithmetic circuit C over a ring R and variables x1 , . . . , xt is a directed acyclic graph whose
gates have in-degree zero or two. The in-degree-zero gates are labeled by variables or elements
of R, and the in-degree-two gates are labeled by + or ×. Unless stated otherwise, a circuit has
one designated output gate and computes a polynomial in R[x1 , . . . , xt ]. Polynomial identities are
understood formally: two polynomials are identical if all their corresponding coefficients agree. The
degree of a circuit means the total degree of its output polynomial.

Size and encodings. We fix binary encodings of the ring elements used as constants. The size of
a circuit C is the number of gates plus the sum of the bit-lengths encoding the constants in the
circuit. We write asizeR (P ) for the minimum size of an arithmetic circuit over R computing P . For
a size bound s(t), we write ASIZER [s(t)] for polynomial families in t variables that admit circuits of
size at most s(t) for all sufficiently large t; we omit the subscript when the ring is clear.

Polynomial identity testing. The problem PITR asks whether the polynomial computed by an
input arithmetic circuit over R is identically zero. For a function f : Z≥1 → R≥0 , Promise f (t)-PITR

promise. For a constant degree bound d, we abbreviate this problem as d-PITR . Note that the
degree bound is a promise only on the output polynomial, and it need not hold at intermediate
gates.

2.2   Boolean Circuits and Satisfiability
A Boolean circuit C on variables x1 , . . . , xn is a directed acyclic graph whose gates have in-degree
zero or two. The in-degree-zero gates are labeled by variables or the constants 0 and 1. Each
in-degree-two gate is labeled by a binary Boolean function, which it applies to its two inputs. Unless
stated otherwise, the circuit has one designated output gate. Its size, denoted by size(C), is its
number of gates. For circuits with multiple output bits, we specify an ordered list of output gates
and include this list in the circuit encoding.
    For a class of languages L and a size bound s(n), we write L ⊆ SIZE[s(n)] if, for every L ∈ L
and all sufficiently large n, the restriction of L to inputs of length n is computed by a Boolean
circuit of size at most s(n). As usual, P/poly = c≥1 SIZE[nc ].
                                                     S

    A formula is a Boolean circuit over {∧, ∨} whose underlying graph is a tree and whose leaves are
labeled by literals or constants. Its size is its number of leaves. A branching program on n inputs is
a directed acyclic graph with a start node and two sinks, labeled accept and reject. Every other
node α is labeled by an index i ∈ [n] and has two outgoing edges leading to nodes α0 and α1 . On
input µ ∈ {0, 1}n , the computation starts at the start node and moves from a node labeled by i to
αµi until it reaches a sink. The size of a branching program is its number of nodes.

Lemma 2.6 ([Weg87]). Every formula of size L on n inputs can be computed by a branching
program of size at most L + 2.

    In k-SAT, the input is a CNF formula with at most k literals per clause, and the task is to
determine whether some assignment satisfies all its clauses. The complementary problem is k-UNSAT.
We remove duplicate clauses, so for fixed k the binary encoding length is polynomial in the number
of variables.
    We use the Tseitin transformation in a form that preserves the number of satisfying assignments.

assignment otherwise.

2.4    Range Avoidance and Nondeterministic Constructions
Range avoidance provides a framework for studying explicit construction problems [Kor21].


                                                    20
Definition 2.10 (Range avoidance). An instance of Avoid is a Boolean circuit C : {0, 1}n → {0, 1}m
for m > n. The task is to find a string y ∈ {0, 1}m outside Range(C) := {C(x) : x ∈ {0, 1}n }. We
write LexAvoid(C) for the lexicographically smallest such string.
    Since m > n, at least one string lies outside the range of C, so LexAvoid(C) is defined on all
instances.
    We say that a function f admits a single-valued FNP construction if there is a nondeterministic
polynomial-time algorithm that, on every input x in the domain of f , has at least one accepting
computation and outputs f (x) on every accepting computation. On inputs outside the domain of f ,
all computations reject. The accepting computations need not be unique; only their output must be
the same. This is the standard notion of a function in NPSV [Sel94, HNOS96].
    The following lemma shows that PH = NP suffices for such a construction of LexAvoid.
Lemma 2.11. If PH = NP, then LexAvoid has a single-valued FNP algorithm.
Proof. Fix a circuit C : {0, 1}n → {0, 1}m with m > n. A string y ∈ {0, 1}m equals LexAvoid(C) if
and only if both of the following conditions hold:
    1. y is outside the range of C: ∀x ∈ {0, 1}n , C(x) ̸= y;
    2. every lexicographically smaller string is in the range of C: ∀z ∈ {0, 1}m satisfying z <lex y,
       ∃x ∈ {0, 1}n such that C(x) = z.
Their conjunction is a ΠP2 predicate. Under PH = NP, this predicate belongs to NP. Hence there
are a polynomial-time verifier V and a polynomial p such that

                  y = LexAvoid(C)      ⇐⇒        ∃w ∈ {0, 1}≤p(|⟨C⟩|) ,     V (C, y, w) = 1.

A nondeterministic algorithm guesses y and w, runs the verifier, and outputs y if the verifier accepts;
otherwise it rejects. The string LexAvoid(C) exists and has an accepting certificate, so at least one
branch accepts. No other string has an accepting certificate, so every accepting branch outputs
LexAvoid(C).

In this subsection, we give the unconditional PIT algorithm that replaces the assumed one in the

Details (elaborating on the points above). For a few years, the authors worked on the idea
that derandomizing PIT even for polynomials of low degree implies circuit lower bounds. Our
initial idea was to use the polynomial formulations of [BGK+ 25, BKM+ 24]. Following this idea,
we obtained a weaker version of Corollary 4 and a slightly weaker version of Corollary 7. Starting

over many rounds of interactive discussion with Claude Opus 5.5, we tried to design a fast PIT
algorithm for restricted classes of arithmetic circuits; this work led to Theorem 5 and, eventually,
to Theorem 1. Thus, the tool materially affected these statements and their proofs in Sections 3, 6,
8 and 9. Nevertheless, the whole text of the paper was written by the authors: we carefully rewrote
all proofs suggested by the model, simplified and cleaned up the proofs, and carefully worked out
the presentation of all results. The authors verified the correctness and originality of all content,
including references. We also used Claude Opus 5.5 to help with finding typos and errors and with
editing the exposition. Below, we state our initial results and compare them with the final ones.
                                                                                              1/3
Constant-degree PIT. Our version of Corollary 4 assumed that D-PIT ∈ NTIME[so(D ) ] for
every constant degree D ≥ 1. It concluded that either ENP requires circuits of size ω(n), or, for
every constant γ ≥ 1, there is an explicit family of constant-degree polynomials on t variables
over Z requiring arithmetic circuits of size Ω(tγ ). GPT 6 Astra derived Corollary 4 from Theorem 3,
a clever fixed-degree statement that it found and proved based on our techniques.

Satisfiability algorithms and Boolean lower bounds. Earlier, we also wanted to use the results
of Murray and Williams [MW18] and Williams [Wil13, Wil14], which turn nontrivial satisfiability
algorithms into circuit lower bounds, but we did not manage to apply them since we had no polynomial
formulation for Circuit-SAT. GPT 6 Astra applied them in a clever way via a reduction to branching
programs. To decide unsatisfiability of a Boolean circuit, its algorithm guesses a formula for every
gate of the circuit, converts each check into a branching program, and verifies the branching programs
by PIT (see Sections 6.2 and 6.3). This gives the Boolean alternatives of Theorem 3 and Corollary 4.

Range avoidance. We proved Corollary 7 ourselves in the following form. If PIT ∈ NP, then
either VP =
          ̸ VNP, or NP = coNP, in which case StrongAvoid ∈ FNP, then ENP requires circuits of size
2n /(2n) [Kor21], and, for every fixed D ≥ 1, there is an FNP-constructible family of polynomials
over Z of degree at most D in VP requiring arithmetic circuits of size ΩD (tD / log t). GPT 6 Astra
suggested an elegant way to shave the logarithmic factor (see Lemma 9.1), which gives the tight
bound Θd (td ) in Corollary 7.
