## 1. The equivalence kernel

The strongest historical lead combines the actual divisor-correspondence construction in cote 44/9, Saito’s Picard-homomorphism definition, and Kleiman’s explicit attribution to an unrecorded 1964 observation of Grothendieck (Saito, 1979, Definition 3.1, Proposition 3.4 and Theorem 3.6; Kleiman, 1971, pp. 446–447). The task would establish which part of the programme has an incidence realization and which statements require universal regular representatives.

The first concrete step is to write, for one smooth projective \(X\), the three kernels on algebraically trivial cycles: annihilation by all Picard correspondences, annihilation by all regular homomorphisms, and annihilation by the algebraic Abel–Jacobi map. Trace the actual family \(z\) and quotient \(A/N\) through those definitions. Any isogeny qualification must remain explicit. The original Lieberman and Murre texts are useful backward-reference targets, but this audit does not pretend their inaccessible originals were read.

## 2. Integral descent before positivity

The elliptic counterexample shows that the full-kernel quotient does not automatically carry an integral pullback of the incidence form. Quotienting by the reduced neutral abelian radical, working in the isogeny category, and rescaling the correspondence are different repairs.

A useful first task is to classify the descent denominator for symmetric integer matrices on a product of elliptic curves and compare the finite lattice quotient with Saito’s complementary dual isogeny (1979, Proposition 4.4). Then separate the factor-two Néron–Severi construction from primitive-sign positivity. Peters’s 2026 conditional comparison supplies a precise boundary; the withdrawn Caibar–Clemens preprint supplies none.

## 3. The unfinished unit comparison

The source constructs an actual map. Its omitted compatibility is therefore a well-bounded companion task. Laumon’s relative theorem fixes the target, determinant line and shift (1996, Lemma 1.2.5).

Place the top-cohomology adjunction map and Koszul self-intersection map in one diagram. Compute their degree-one normalization from the Poincaré class and check the higher exterior products. A perfect-cone argument can finish only after this map-level compatibility is checked. Matching binomial dimensions is insufficient.

## 4. Oda and the restricted Lie extension

The Hodge-extension question has an exact later answer over a perfect field, with a documented June 1967 dissertation before Oda’s 1969 publication. The remaining task is to align the source’s restricted \(p\)-operation with Oda’s semilinear \(F, V\) and dual-variety conventions.

The first step is a two-column table for \(A\), \(A^\vee\), their universal vector extensions, Frobenius and Verschiebung kernels, Cartier duality and \(M(A[p])\). Distinguish the Cartier dual of a Frobenius kernel from its annihilator inside the full \(p\)-torsion pairing. Keep cote 43’s actual statements separate from its later bibliography and comparison notes.

## 5. The Tate strengthening

The abstract \(\mathbb Z_\ell\)-module strengthening is formal because \(T_\ell J\) is finite free. The substantive task is to identify what structure an inclusion should preserve.

Search nearby correspondence for naturality, correspondences, equivariance or saturation language, then compare that exact formulation with Achter, Casalaina-Martin and Vial’s Proposition 8.2 (2023). Retain its excluded primes and target modulo torsion. One run can settle the logical alternatives and work a low-codimension case; an integral realization in every codimension is a different task.

## 6. The relative exponential and integral filtration

The corrected ample-field coefficients and the surviving final integral multiple are fixed. The remaining questions concern the family formula and the meaning of “mod torsion.”

First identify the filtration denoted by \(\mathrm{Gr}\) from the surrounding archive. Then derive the family statement with index, determinant and Todd factors visible, comparing it with Moonen’s Todd identity (2026), Beckmann and de Gaay Fortman’s Theorem 3.8 (2023), and Hasan et al.’s localized-coefficient result (2025). These are different levels of integrality; a broad label must not erase their distinctions.

## 7. The height formula

A surface pencil offers a controlled sign and normalization test. The known divisor formula uses a regular fibred model and vertically orthogonal corrected divisors; raw intersection on the original surface is not automatically the same expression.

The first step is to blow up a specified pencil, compute its fibre-component correction matrix, and compare corrected negative intersection with geometric Jacobian height. Ulmer’s corrected finite/algebraically closed setting is suitable (2014, §4.3 and errata). Künnemann’s abelian-number-field result (2001, Corollary 1.7) belongs to a subsequent higher-cycle comparison with its own hypotheses.

## 8. The τ annotation

Under the usual interpretation, the divisor theorem is known and the unrestricted higher-cycle statement is false. The useful next task is a source decision.

Inspect the symbol, nearby qualifiers and the programme’s relation to the letter, then compare with FGA VI §4, p. 242. Neither Matsusaka’s name nor the modern counterexample licenses adding “divisors” to the transcription. An alternative intended relation must be recovered before assigning its fate.
