# K78-04 · Mathematical notes

## What the pages do

**A pinned object over the prime field (47).** The author seeks an épinglage for the objects abbreviated SRnS. He hopes that descent will then give a canonical object over the prime field and that the others will arise by twisting by a torsor. He asks for a section of the gerbe, allowing nonsplit configurations whose vertex subscheme has no points.

**Three integral base rings (47–49).** A list assigns ℤ[1/n] to (a), ℤ to (b) for an odd prime or 4, and ℤ[1/2] to (c) for twice an odd prime. Scan 48 writes down a subgroup, an exchange matrix and the point (1,1), then identifies a model and its automorphism scheme. Returning to a prime field in 49 does not dispose of the questions over the two smaller rings. The attempted construction underneath is cancelled.

**Course outline (50–51).** The cover opens part II. The first outline item gives two presentations of the finite and infinite dihedral groups. The other items concern affine transformations and quadratic metrics. Scan 54 is an unused printed letterhead between sets of notes.

**Realisation and incidence (51–52).** Vertices S are points of the affine plane E and edges A are lines. A realisation of an abstract polygon maps its vertices and edges into these two sets. Distinct images of adjacent vertices determine the relevant edge map. The strengthened reverse incidence implication defines a faithful realisation.

**Regularity via rotations (52).** In the finite metric case the author uses the centre of gravity, then a rotation ζ sending one vertex to the next. Its powers give the vertices and the lines through consecutive vertices give the edges. The oblique note identifies the vertex and edge sets as torsors. Allowing the whole Euclidean automorphism group brings the flags into the transitivity statement.

**Recovering a metric (53).** The author now starts with affine automorphisms transitive on flags. He proposes to recover the metric up to homothety and allows it to degenerate. The triangle and the parallelogram illustrate how this usage of “regular” depends on the metric. Two numbered lines start a formal definition.

**Incidence data and contours (55).** A polygon is described by S, A and a flag relation R. The two projections have degree 2 and the incidence structure is connected. Dropping connectedness gives a contour. Further changes to the conditions lead to a graph and a combinatorial one-dimensional cell complex.

**Two involutions on flags (55–56).** The fibres of the two projections specify fixed-point-free involutions. The flag set acquires an action of the free product of two groups of order 2. The admissibility conditions separate a flag from its two neighbours. Scan 56 changes generators and writes the infinite dihedral group as a semidirect product.

**Stabilizers of a connected contour (57).** A point stabilizer H must avoid specified translations and reflections, including in conjugate stabilizers. The author obtains the trivial subgroup or nℤ with n at least 2. His computation splits the reflections into two conjugacy classes according to the parity of an exponent.

**The models and their flags (58–59).** For Πₙ the vertex and edge sets are indexed by ℤₙ. The two flags on edge aᵢ use sᵢ and sᵢ₊₁. A drawing marks the first two edges. Evaluation of an isomorphism at a flag is asserted to give a bijection. This yields a model with a chosen flag and the canonical versus noncanonical descriptions of the automorphism group.

**The small polygons (59–60).** The text relates the extra condition on the two involutions to the exclusion of a one-sided polygon. It then asks whether a requirement on least upper bounds, carried over from polyhedra, would also remove the digon. The continuation adopts n ≥ 3.

**Orientations (60).** A subset R⁺ of the flags picks one flag over each vertex and each edge. Each involution exchanges this subset with its complement. The corresponding graph is a bijection between vertices and edges. The author gives a product rule for disjoint unions and identifies the two orientations of a polygon with the two orbits of the rotation subgroup.

## Remarks of the model

**R48 · Scan 48 · Model remark, high confidence.** The displayed exchange matrix has determinant −1 over ℤ. This refutes its membership in the displayed special linear group. Its entries do give an invertible integral matrix. The discrepancy concerns this specific inclusion.

**R57 · Scan 57 · Model remark, high confidence for the check.** Taking u(x)=x+1 and σ(x)=−x on the integers, both conjugation expressions evaluate to the indicated translations of the reflection: 2m−x and 2m+1−x. The numerical check covered 121 pairs (m,x), with each coordinate from −5 through 5.

**R58 · Scans 56, 58 · Model remark, low confidence about the convention.** For translations and reflection modulo 3 and 4, uσ and σu take 0 to different vertices. This makes their order worth recording at S58. The cramped annotation and overwritten earlier formula leave open the possibility of a change in how the two reflections are identified.

**R59a · Scans 58–59 · Model remark, high confidence for n=2,3,4,5.** Using the two incidence pairs written on 58, I enumerated the vertex and edge permutations preserving incidence. The counts were 4, 6, 8 and 10. In each case the images of a single flag were all distinct and exhausted the flags, as the evaluation corollary predicts.

**R59b · Scans 59–60 · Model remark, high confidence for the digon.** Its two vertices have two distinct common incident edges. In the order consisting of vertices below incident edges there is no least common upper bound for that vertex pair. The maximum number of common edges for distinct vertices in the tested 3-, 4- and 5-gons was one. This gives a concrete reason for the author’s lower-bound discussion.

**R60 · Scan 60 · Model remark, high confidence for the finite examples.** Counting subsets which project bijectively onto vertices and onto edges gives two orientations for each of the same four models. The disjoint union of a digon and a triangle gives four, agreeing with the displayed product rule.

**Reading question · Scan 60, near line 12.** The frozen and final text read « c’est ainsi une partie ». The alternative « c’est aussi une partie » deserves consideration: both middle letters form a low joined group, with no decisive separate point. This question does not affect the definition.
