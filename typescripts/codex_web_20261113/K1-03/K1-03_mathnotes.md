# Mathematical notes on scans 79–92

The numbers in parentheses are the numbers on the physical sheets. “Prefix” means the first $k$ entries of the finite or infinite sequence in question. Stable identifiers link observations here to CHECK_EVENTS.tsv.

## What the pages do

**M01 · Fredholm coefficients, scan 79.** Corollaires 4 and 5 compare the coefficients of $\det(1+zu)$ with the corresponding positive-operator coefficients, then compare the determinants. The proof uses the trace of an induced operator. The order of the two factors under one radical is doubtful.

**M02 · Eigenvalues, determinant and trace, scans 79–80.** A cancelled theorem expresses the Fredholm determinant by a product over eigenvalues. The turned note links its coefficients to elementary symmetric sums and its first coefficient to the trace. The draft considers orthogonal projection onto a span of eigenvectors, the zeros of the entire determinant and the expansion in rank-one tensors for a positive operator. Its last calculation groups tuples that differ only by their order.

**M03 · Supporting affine functions, scan 81.** The author places a hyperplane through a boundary point of the strict epigraph of a convex function. He writes its affine equation, compares its height with the function and describes the function as the supremum of such supports. For monotonicity he varies one coordinate at a time. A small curve and a marked point accompany this argument.

**M04 · Two forms of the convexity theorem, scan 82.** The theorem marked “(H. Weyl)” starts from decreasing $\alpha_i$ and upper bounds on their prefix sums. It compares the values of a convex symmetric function under either coordinatewise monotonicity or equality of the full sums. The multiplicative form uses prefix products and convexity after exponentiating the arguments. For strictly positive entries the change of variables is logarithmic.

**M05 · Boundary points, scans 82–83.** The author approximates admissible nonnegative systems by positive ones for the increasing-function case. Several longer constructions involving primed and double-primed entries are struck. The shorter surviving prescription keeps the rho sequence and adds epsilon to the sigma sequence at this stage of the argument.

**M06 · Proof of the additive comparison, scans 83–85.** Affine supports reduce the comparison of functions to a comparison of weighted sums. Symmetry permits permutations of a support. The rearrangement lemma places the coefficients in decreasing order. Formula (9) then writes the weighted sum as a combination of prefix sums. The last coefficient explains the separate roles of monotonicity and equality of the totals.

**M07 · Reciprocal sequences, scan 85.** After (9) a corollary compares the function on two lists of reciprocals. The argument divides the equal full products by prefix products and reverses the order of the entries. The written directions of comparison matter here, both in the proof and in (10). See S11–S12.

**M08 · Truncation and norms, scans 86–87.** The first remark passes to shorter sequences for the increasing-function comparison. A marginal converse tests a function inequality against sums over subsets of indices. The examples include an increasing convex function composed with exponentials and a normalized symmetric norm invariant under changes of sign. The margin discusses monotonicity of the norm by means of its unit ball. A later marginal formula pads the shorter vector with zeros.

**M09 · Separable functions and powers, scans 87–88.** The single sheet at scan 88 builds a function of several variables by summing one-variable functions and applies the two theorem hypotheses to full sums and prefixes. The choice $f(s)=s^p$ gives $f(e^t)=e^{pt}$. These are the examples associated with (13)–(16), whose numbers run across the two physical sheets in a different order. An unfinished converse is crossed out on scan 87.

**M10 · Eigenvalues and singular values, scans 87 and 89.** The first Hilbert-space convexity theorem compares $f(|\lambda_1|,\ldots,|\lambda_n|)$ with $f(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_n)$ for a compact operator. Corollaries apply it to prefix sums and their limits, to positive powers and to the Fredholm and Hilbert–Schmidt cases. The two separate formulae numbered (21) are retained in the transcription.

**M11 · Determinants and extension of norms, scans 89–91.** In finite dimension the equality of full products permits the comparison without coordinatewise monotonicity, subject to invertibility or boundary continuity. The turned calculation on scan 90 identifies the singular-value product with the absolute determinant. A second corollary compares finite-dimensional Schatten norms. For a sequence $\xi$, scan 91 defines $N(\xi)$ by the increasing limit of the norms of its zero-padded truncations, allowing an infinite limit.

**M12 · Products of compact operators, scan 91.** The second Hilbert-space convexity theorem compares a function of the singular values of a composite with the same function of the coordinatewise products of the two singular-value lists. The corollary gives finite and infinite sum inequalities. A crossed strip below the main page discusses multiplication operators and a further corollary left to the reader. The written orders $uv$ and $vu$ need attention wherever the two letters nearly coincide.

**M13 · Hölder and the trace, scan 92.** The long turned insertion specializes the sum comparison, gives a sufficient condition for the composite to be Fredholm and writes a bound on its trace. The main text applies scalar Hölder to obtain bounds in terms of two power norms, then singles out conjugate exponents and the trace norm. The marginal corollary number is uncertain.

**M14 · Equal finite dimensions, scan 92.** The lower slip removes monotonicity under an invertibility or continuity hypothesis. Its proof identifies the full product in (40) with the absolute determinant of the composite and the product of the two absolute determinants. A limiting argument covers boundary points. The final pencil line is an instruction about composition of operators.

## Remarks of the model

**R01 · Scan 85, formula (9). Model, high confidence.** The two-term case shows what the differences of adjacent coefficients are doing. With $a=(5,2)$ and $\alpha=(3,1)$, the weighted sum and the prefix expression both equal 17. This is one direct computation on the displayed identity.

**R02 · Scans 90–92, full products and prefixes. Model, high confidence.** Let $u$ and $v$ be diagonal with entries $(3,2)$ and $(1,5)$. The composite has singular values $10,3$. Its first singular value is strictly below $3\cdot5=15$, while its full product $30$ equals $(3\cdot2)(5\cdot1)$. Thus the full-product equality used on the last slip can coexist with a strict prefix comparison. The example also distinguishes the two hypotheses cited around (29).

**R03 · Scan 92, lower slip, last proof. Reading question, low confidence.** The frozen transcription reads “(formule (16))”. “(formule (26))” is a possible reading of the first digit and points to the marginal determinant identity on scan 90. The internal reference alone cannot decide a faint figure.

**R04 · Scans 91–92, repeated operator letters. Reading question, low confidence.** The first argument in the frozen (29) reads $\rho_1(\mathrm{uv}|\mathrm{vu})$, with $uv$ first. The two letters are joined at that site. The order in words, an application from $E$ to $F$ followed by one from $F$ to $G$, clarifies the composite meant, while leaving the writer's choice of letter order open.

**R05 · Scan 85, (10). Model, low confidence about the intended revision.** Under the equal-product, prefix-product hypotheses, the reciprocal example in S12 gives a corrected numerical comparison $1\leq5/4$. The displayed order gives the reverse. Here both original lists are positive, decreasing and have product 4, and the test function is the sum of the coordinates. The numerical counterexample is exact. The interpretation of the revised case label is less secure.

**R06 · Scan 79, opening line of Corollaire 4. Reading question, low confidence.** The frozen text reads “d'un espace de Hilbert”. “dans l'espace de Hilbert” is another possible division of the strokes after “Fredholm”. The first downstroke is tall, but the following letters are compressed before “espace”. Either wording fits the sentence.

**R07 · Scan 86, first example below the crossed heading on convexity. Reading question, low confidence.** The frozen text has $\rho_i\geq0$. Another reading is $\rho_i>0$. A small stroke below the angle is hard to separate from the adjacent writing. The later discussion includes zero among the norm arguments, which favours the first reading without settling the shape here.
