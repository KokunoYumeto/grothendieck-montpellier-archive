# Mathematical notes on cote 74, scans 73–88

## What the pages do

**73. Coverings attached to hexagons.** The opening list distinguishes \(\mathrm{Hex}\), the large hexagons, from \(\mathrm{hex}\), the small ones. It sets these beside pavés, pairs and triples. The square in mid-page puts 2 on its leftward maps and 3 on its downward maps. The author explains how the shorter chain \(\mathrm{Tria}'\to\mathrm{Hex}\to\mathrm{triHex}\) recovers the associated principal covering. The last line recovers \(\Delta\) from a fibre regarded as an ensemble of type \((3,3,3)\). The first numerical coefficient and several names are doubtful.

**74–75. From a small hexagon to its complementary triple.** Starting with the six labelled vertices of \(h\), the author takes the pavé \(P\) containing it. His third triple is \(P-h\). Its elements are paired with the diagonals of \(h\); the margin describes a second construction using opposite sides. Conversely, deleting the triple from its pavé returns a hexagon. This raises the question of the large hexagon attached to the same triple.

**75–76. Data for the two hexagons.** A combinatorial hexagon gives a three-element set \(\tau\) of diagonals and a two-element set \(\varepsilon\) of inscribed triangles. The text writes down adjacency on \(\tau\times\varepsilon\) and compares orientations. In terms of the family \(I\to\Lambda\) it writes \(h\Leftrightarrow(I_\gamma,I_\alpha\setminus\{i\})\), then describes the large hexagon \(H\) using \(I_\alpha\). It distinguishes the orientation sets of \(h\) and \(H\).

**76–77. A symmetric description and duality.** The “Digression” adjoins \(\varepsilon'\), the two *coinscrits* triangles, and an isomorphism \(\varepsilon\wedge\varepsilon'\simeq\omega(\tau)\). It gives the vertices, the edges and their incidence in these data. Duality exchanges \(\varepsilon\) and \(\varepsilon'\) and changes the map to \(-\varphi\circ\sigma\). The automorphism group is described by a sign condition. Several of the sign variables are overwritten. A diagram relates the resulting sets, including the sets of diagonals, orientations and frames.

**78. The landscape sheet.** The top-right key gives the sizes \(3,2,2,2,6,6,12\) for the diagonals, two sorts of triangles, orientations, vertices, edges and frames, in the order written there. The large network compares marked and oriented hexagons, pavés and triples by forgetting markings or taking opposites. A smaller diagram and a list of subgroups occupy the lower right. The chart has lines crossing labels and cancellations, especially in the middle. Its local connections are less certain than the sizes in the key.

**79. Polygons with three and four sides.** For a triangle the author identifies vertices and sides by opposition and extends the observation to odd polygons. For a square he partitions the sides into opposite pairs. Vertices can then be described by sections of the resulting two fibres. The cube and its legend relate frames, diagonals, codiagonals and orientations. The last paragraph interprets stabilizers in the symmetry group \(D_4\); the lower margin qualifies the count.

**80–81. The hexagon and \(D_6\).** The author returns to the \(3\)-by-\(2\) description, identifies each diagonal with the pair of sides parallel to it, and compares inscribed and circumscribed or coinscribed triangles. The diagram is labelled with maps of degrees two and three. The list below and its continuation on 81 discuss the stabilizers of marked objects, their conjugacy classes, and the automorphisms of the corresponding homogeneous spaces. It distinguishes the central involution from the two classes of other involutions.

**82–83. A square in a pavé.** A square \(C\) determines its centre \(a\), the vertex unlinked to its four vertices. Marking a diagonal or a vertex of the square corresponds to pointing or pinning one of the triples. The four points outside \(C\cup\{a\}\) are related to pairs of points on the two diagonals. The “ombre” of each outside point on \(C\) gives a side. The page goes on to marked sides, codiagonals and frames. A turned diagram in the right margin gives the corresponding hexagon objects.

**84. Envelopes of finite graphs.** The author asks which finite graphs admit an “enveloppe”. The inserted qualifier and the word after it need care. Examples begin with a point, a segment and polygon outlines. Two solid sketches are crossed out. Below the long dividing line, an exploratory diagram compares marked squares and hexagons, with small marked configurations beside the terms. The precise captions of the round and double-lobed shapes are doubtful.

**85–86. The anticarré and \(\mathrm{Pav}(C)\).** An anticarré is the opposite graph of a square, a sum of two segments. The sheet associates the square with an anticarré by removing the square and its centre, and uses that other graph to describe \(\mathrm{Pav}(C)\). The next sheet compares the opposite pavé to \(\mathrm{Pav}(C^*)\). Restriction to the square gives a stated universal property for embeddings. Its consequence is phrased as a correspondence with pointed pavés. The final NB distinguishes the four pavés containing an anticarré in a cubical graph.

**87. Product, opposite and coproduct of graphs.** Two vertices in the product are linked when a single coordinate moves along an edge and all others agree. Opposition complements adjacency between distinct vertices. The author distinguishes an involution on graph structures on a fixed set from the possibility of an isomorphism to the opposite graph. Applying opposition to the product of the opposite factors defines the coproduct. The final proposition applies these operations to a pavé.

**88. Four labelled vertices.** Two doublets, two segments, and a doublet with a segment furnish examples. The same four labels are retained while the links change. The two configurations marked “carré” are drawn differently. Crossed diagonals in the first drawing and the partly overwritten heading matter to its interpretation. The last line begins to enumerate three natural square structures. The batch ends during this enumeration.

## Remarks of the model

**R1 — model, high confidence.** Applying the adjacency test on scan 75 to all pairs in a three-by-two set gives six vertices and six edges. Every vertex has degree two and the graph is connected. The explicit test therefore recovers a single hexagon.

**R2 — model, high confidence in the enumeration.** For the twelve symmetries of a hexagon, enumeration gives seven subgroups of order 2, three of order 4 and three of order 6. At *indices* 2, 4 and 6 the counts are three, one and seven. The order-two groups fall into conjugacy classes of sizes one, three and three. This also explains why “3 classes” is a plausible reading of the small parenthesis on 78.

**R3 — model, high confidence.** Enumeration of the eight symmetries of a square gives ten subgroups and eight conjugacy classes of subgroups. The central subgroup of order two is alone in its class. The other four subgroups of order two form two pairs. This gives a precise reading of how the margin qualifies the last paragraph of 79.

**R4 — model, high confidence for the finite example.** Using the definition of a pavé on 87, I enumerated the four-vertex induced squares in the product of two triangles. There are nine. Each has exactly one outside vertex adjacent to none of its vertices, and those nine centres are distinct. This checks the opening correspondence of 82 in the graph defined on these sheets.

**R5 — model, high confidence for the labelled graphs.** The four labels on 88 admit three perfect matchings. Each complement is a four-cycle, giving the three distinct square structures. With the operations of 87, two segments have a four-edge product and a six-edge coproduct. Two doublets have a two-edge coproduct. The mixed product has two edges and its opposite has four. This checks the count while leaving the overwritten heading open.

**R6 — model, reading question; medium confidence in the alternative.** At 73, line 5 of the list, the frozen marked reading *trièdres* acquired the alternative *triades*. A return to the short word on 74 near “elle est contenue”, the sentence on 75 near “celles données”, and 82 near “d’une trièdre pointée” favours *triade* at least in these passages. The cluster between *tri* and the final *e* is compressed. The unquestionably feminine endings and the marked compound settled as *bitriade* on 80 support that alternative. Numerous unmarked occurrences of *trièdre(s)* are therefore questionable. Inferring a gender error by the author would be unsafe.

**R7 — model, reading question; low confidence.** On 77 the condition above the large diagram writes a corrected pair of signs on the left, while the expression after “ou” has \(\alpha\beta\). The sign argument in the first expression was frozen as \(\tau\) or \(u\). One possibility is that the second expression preserves an earlier choice of variable names. The crossing strokes prevent a secure assignment of the signs in the earlier version.

**R8 — model, correction to the drawing on scan 84; high confidence.** In the upper row of examples, the two outlines immediately after the square were both frozen as five-sided in TikZ. On the crop the first has six turns, at the top, upper right, lower right, bottom, lower left and upper left. The following outline has five. The sequence on the manuscript is square, hexagon, pentagon. The missing corner in the first TikZ outline lies outside the marked witness spans.
