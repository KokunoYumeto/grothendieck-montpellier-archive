## What the pages do

**267–268 · Relative locally constant types.** For each base $S$, a chosen full subcategory $F_{0S}$ lies inside the category of fibrations. Inclusions through an intermediate full subcategory are to induce equivalences after localisation. The choice of $F_{0S}$ is treated as additional structure. The next sheet formulates a variant using the whole category of fibrations and asks how the circled conditions are related.

**268–270 · A finer relative localiser.** Section 5 turns to relative homotopy types that may vary over the base. A second class $W_S^0$ gives a category $\mathrm{Ho}_W(S)$. In Cat, the operation sending a category to its opposite exchanges two choices, denoted by upper $g$ and $d$. Grothendieck selects a class $L$ of arrows, called localisation arrows or local isomorphisms, and specifies closure under composition and base change. A map over $S$ passes the $W_S^L$ test when its pullback after each permitted change of base lies in $W$.

**270–271 · Inverse images for varying bases.** The manuscript puts $W_S^L$ between the universal and underlying localisers. On fibrations the three coincide under the earlier axiom. For arbitrary $S'\to S$, the desired inverse-image functor should act on the new localised categories. The comparison from fibrations now looks to the author like an inclusion of locally constant types, described as “pl. fid”.

**272–273 · Saturating the testing class.** The initial definition collects maps whose base-change functor preserves $W^g$. Grothendieck strengthens the requirement by testing after every further base change $T\to S$. He states stability under composition and base change, inclusion of the original class, and equality of the corresponding localisers. In Cat he puts the $W$-smooth arrows inside the enlarged class, leaving equality open. This gives an alternative description of $W_S^g$.

**273–274 · The two localisation presentations.** On the $g$ side the displayed equivalence uses the full subcategory of proper maps over $S$. The $d$ side uses the smooth maps. Restriction to the appropriate presenting subcategory gives the comparisons with the universal localiser. The argument distinguishes the inverse-image operation available on a localisation from passage to fractions directly on the entire slice.

**274–276 · A proposal for other categories of models.** Grothendieck asks how generally smooth and proper arrows could fill these two roles. Opposites in a presheaf example and the pair “propreté” and “asphéricité locale” in topology provide possible tests. He considers giving classes Liss and Prop from the start. On 276 he sets out the two localisers, their behaviour under change of base, a pair of localisation equivalences and the corresponding adjunction questions.

**277 · From fibres to cofibres.** Section 6 asks for a dual theory in Cat. Open and closed Dwyer immersions are the available examples. He assumes $W(7\ \mathrm{bis})$ for this section. A sideways note spells out the cofibration claim, the two weak-equivalence tests for a square and stability of open Dwyer immersions.

**278–280 · Factorisation by a cylinder.** Grothendieck constructs an interval from an alternating finite string with $n\geq2$, and includes the discrete two-point category at its endpoints. Product with $X$ gives the cylinder and a factorisation of the codiagonal. A pushout along one endpoint defines $Z(f)$; the other endpoint gives the first map in the factorisation of $f$. The expanded diagram identifies it as a composite of open Dwyer immersions. For the map $Z(f)\to Y$, he uses its retraction relation with $Y\to Z(f)$.

**280–281 · Proposition 16.** On the undercategory of $W$-cofibrations out of $S$, the proposition asserts that weak equivalences remain so after every pushout along $S\to S'$. The proof factors this base map and introduces a three-by-three diagram. It obtains $f'_0\in W$ before the manuscript breaks off during the next sentence.
