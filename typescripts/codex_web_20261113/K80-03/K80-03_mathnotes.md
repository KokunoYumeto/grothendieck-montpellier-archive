# K80-03 · Mathematical notes

## What the pages do

**Scan 39.** The page starts midway through the description of a cube of categories and functors. Grothendieck identifies the oriented and unoriented faces, the spherical and projective faces, and the faces of two-dimensional configurations and their associated graphs. The arrow decorations distinguish equivalences, faithful functors, and surjectivity on sets of morphisms.

**Scans 40–41.** Each nonconcurrent triple of pseudo-lines has an associated graph. An orientation of the one-dimensional arrangement chooses an orientation of this graph for every such triple. A combinatorial map of a pseudo-line arrangement gives an oriented arrangement on its 1-skeleton, the functor (18). The next page specifies the incidence hypotheses, including one common vertex for each pair of lines (B1). In the subarrangement on a subset of lines, only their pairwise intersections are retained as vertices.

**Scans 42–43.** To reconstruct a map, he seeks two inverse cyclic orders on the arcs issuing from every vertex, together with transport of these orders along each oriented edge. Reversing the edge inverts its transport, (19)–(21). To recover the given lines, a half-turn in the cyclic order must take an oriented line to its reverse, (22). The projective-plane requirement brings in connectedness and the face count (23). He writes the vertex-minus-edge count as $1-c(K)$ (B2).

**Scans 43–45.** A transversal $\Delta$ missing a vertex $s$ meets each line through $s$. The intersections inherit a polygonal order on $\Delta$, giving an order on the unoriented lines through $s$. Condition (A1) requires this structure to be independent of $\Delta$. Over each such line lie its two orientations, (24)–(27). A lift of a cyclic permutation is specified by bijections between these two-element fibres (B3). The text counts $2^{\nu_s-1}$ cyclic lifts and uses (28) to describe the choice of the last bijection.

**Scans 46–47.** For an oriented transversal, the oriented triangle formed with two successive lines through $s$ prescribes how to pass between their orientation fibres. Figure (29) identifies the edges and intersection points. Condition (A2) asks for a cyclic permutation on the oriented lines. Reversing the transversal gives the inverse permutation in (30), and (A3) asks that the unordered pair be independent of the transversal.

**Scans 48–50.** Transversals missing both ends of an edge map to the two local orientation sets. Condition (A4) says that equality of their induced orientations at one end gives equality at the other, (31)–(32). When no common transversal exists, a triangle supplies a route through a third vertex, (33), and its two edge transports are composed in (34). A proposed (A5) concerns the choices in this construction. The exceptional configuration consists of concurrent lines and a single transversal. Its treatment has a qualification in the body and a reply in the margin.

**Scans 50–52.** To describe a triangle’s orientation from the one-dimensional arrangement, the first case uses a fourth line through none of the triangle’s vertices. An equivalence for simple arrangements of four lines supplies an orientation on the triple. The condition labelled (A5) on scan 52 asks for independence of this fourth line.

**Scans 52–54.** In the second case each further line passes through a triangle vertex. Three nonconcurrent auxiliary lines, one through each vertex, produce six lines and nine marked vertices. Two patterns of opposite vertices give different compatibility rules for the three chosen edges. Condition (A6) requires an allowed pattern and independence of the auxiliary choice. When the three auxiliary lines always concur, the text identifies a six-line arrangement with four triple points and three further crossings (B4). The last page names the automorphism group of the corresponding two-dimensional arrangement and announces a further description.
