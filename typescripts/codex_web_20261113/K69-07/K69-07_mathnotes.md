# Mathematical notes on cote 69, scans 34–40 and 42–49

## What the pages do

**C1. A pair of triangles, scan 34.** A pair meeting at a vertex determines a choice of two among the five triangles at that vertex. The displayed counts give 270 choices, a stabilizer of order 192, and the product identified on the sheet with the order of the Weyl group of type E₆.

**C2. The prism and the bitriangle, scans 35–36.** One configuration is reduced to a square with a marked side. Extending the lateral edges of a triangular prism into triangles produces a nine-vertex bitriangle with a distinguished disjoint pair. Two transverse partitions into triples describe the nine vertices. The six constituent triangles themselves fall into two families of three. The automorphism calculation for the bitriangle gives 72; a further factor of order 6 appears for its embedding in the cubic complex.

**C3. Recovering the ambient graph, scans 36–38.** Three additional triangles through each bitriangle vertex are to be identified compatibly. An outside vertex then carries two coordinates, a three-element subset of the bitriangle and a label from a three-element set. Scan 37 states when two such coordinates are adjacent. Scan 38 proposes testing the resulting graph against the earlier characterization of a cubic complex.

**C4. Bases disjoint from the bitriangle, scans 38–39.** Grothendieck associates bases to injections of a two-element set, constructed using the fibrewise cyclic permutations and a Baer product, into the set of three labels. He describes the inverse construction from a partition into two triples. He counts 720 incident pairs of a basis and an orthogonal bitriangle, with six possible bases over each bitriangle and ten bitriangles over each basis. He calls the partition construction an « autodualité ».

**C5. Roots and relative positions, scan 40.** The differences between a displayed basis and its dual give six roots described as a system of type A₂. The author then sorts the 45 triangles by the size of their intersection with a fixed bitriangle. He starts a separate classification for bases and asks for one for two bitriangles.

**C6. Lines on the blown-up plane, scans 42–44.** The exceptional curves, transforms of the fifteen joining lines, and transforms of six conics give the families of six, fifteen and six. Grothendieck writes their classes and intersection products. Cubics through the six points yield the map to projective three-space. The class λ has square 3 and pairs to 1 with each of the 27 classes. He reads these products as statements about the surface and its lines.

**C7. Abstracting the coordinates, scans 44–46.** Over a nonzero ring the two primed and unprimed families are related by symmetric identities. Scan 45 distinguishes the equality of two classes from the vanishing of four times their difference. It compares the generating families and their bilinear forms. The 27 distinguished elements become vertices, with adjacency determined by product 1.

**C8. Edges and a graph module, scans 46–47.** The written lists give ten neighbours of each vertex. Four types of adjacent pairs lead to four identifications of their unique common neighbour. For a graph the author puts −1 on each diagonal entry of a symmetric form, 1 on edges and 0 on the other off-diagonal entries. Dividing the free vertex module by the kernel gives the module denoted by a hatted E.

**C9. Ordered and unordered bases, scans 48–49.** Isomorphisms from the standard graph parametrize ordered bases. Quotienting by the permutations of six labels gives the underlying basic subsets. The proof recovers each remaining vertex from its neighbours among the labelled vertices and their primed partners. Thus an automorphism fixing the six labels individually fixes the graph.

**C10. The invariant class, scan 49.** The proposition characterizes λ by having product 1 with every vertex. Five identities express it in basis coordinates or in sums over vertices. Pairing with λ gives the map from the hatted module to the integers in the final exact sequence. Its kernel is the orthogonal of λ. The last sentence breaks off while discussing that orthogonal.

## Remarks of the model

**M1. Model remark, high confidence.** The two manuscripts offer independent ways to check scan 40(a). In the integral coordinates of 42–46 I joined two distinct vertices when their product was 1. The resulting graph has 135 edges and 45 triangles. For each of the 120 nine-vertex bitriangles, exactly 6 triangles meet it in three vertices, 27 meet it in one, and 12 miss it. This reproduces the handwritten column in order.

**M2. Model remark, high confidence for the finite count.** In the same graph I enumerated six-element sets of mutually nonadjacent vertices. There are 72. For any fixed bitriangle, 6 of those bases miss all six of its constituent triangles, 54 miss exactly two, and 12 miss none. In the middle case the two missed triangles have five vertices in their union. This tests the numerical description proposed on 40(b) for bases. It leaves open whether these conditions classify positions up to automorphism, and how the word « bibase » is to be used there.

**M3. Model remark, high confidence.** The coordinates allow a short check of the surjection on 49. Pairing λ with even one exceptional class gives 1 over the integers; the displayed map therefore reaches the generator of ℤ. The six exceptional classes and the class of the line through the first two points already generate the seven coordinate directions. The line class plus those first two exceptional classes recovers η.
