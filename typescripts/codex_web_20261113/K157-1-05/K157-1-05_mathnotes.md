# Mathematical notes · cote 157-1, scans 066–081

The scan numbers below identify the supplied images. Page numbers in quotations are Grothendieck’s manuscript numbers. The uncancelled text, later additions and struck attempts sometimes give different accounts of the same statement.

## What the pages do

**066 · Th. 3.** A cartesian square gives the base-change morphism from pullback after direct image to direct image after pullback. The two hypotheses are written with “D-propre” and “D-lisse”. The letter immediately before the latter is overwritten. The proof refers back to an earlier page.

**066–068 · Comparing a category with its opposite.** The construction with a looped capital, transcribed as script D with P as an alternative, has the arrows of X for its objects. A morphism between two arrows uses one map at each end, in opposite directions, subject to the displayed composition law. Source and target give the two projections. Replacing X by its opposite exchanges them. The notes then identify their fibres and slices. To prove a contractibility assertion the writer passes to a category T with initial object o and final object e and singles out the arrow from o to e. The last square exhibits the required morphism to it.

**069 · Proposition 1 and two corollaries.** The projections are called strict Hot-equivalences. Their fibres are described as contractible. The corollaries compare contractibility of X with that of its opposite and equivalence of f with equivalence of its opposite. Functoriality of the construction supplies two commutative squares. The final paragraph names a domain condition on the derivator.

**070–071 · Proposition 1 on D-fibrations and Definition 1.** The section starts a new sequence of proposition numbers. It relates fibre-to-slice inclusions and transition maps along arrows of Y to the direct image of a pulled-back constant coefficient. The latter is to be locally constant and computable fibre by fibre. The proof compares the displayed cohomology groups at objects and along arrows. The dual description uses the other slices and the lower-exclamation direct image. The two definitions are called cohomological or left, and homological or right.

**071–072 · Trying a change of base.** A category over the interval is described by an open subcategory and its closed complement. Grothendieck writes out a condition for a new base to be smooth over the interval, then tests a base made by amalgamating two copies of the interval. He identifies the resulting total category as an amalgam. He concludes that this candidate seems to fail as a counterexample.

**073–075 · Proposition 2 and Definition 2.** Part (a) compares a base change p with the resulting q on total categories. Parts (b) and (b′) concern cohomological and homological fibrations that persist under further base changes. The forward argument tests a pulled-back map on objects and arrows in slice categories. The attempted converse lifts the problem to cohomology with a locally constant coefficient. The marginal objections and cancellations are essential to the status of the asserted equivalence. The text proceeds by duality and gives the name “D-fibration” in Definition 2.

**075–076 · Proposition 3.** Part (a) states stability under base change and composition. A separate note questions the composition claim. Part (b) treats a morphism between two objects over the same base which gives equivalences on the fibres. The proof passes from cohomology of the total categories to a comparison of direct images and then evaluates it fibre by fibre. The note on the left points ahead to proposition 6.

**076 · General coefficients.** Grothendieck asks for fibrewise calculation of both composites on an arbitrary F. For the ordinary direct image he singles out coefficients obtained as a direct image along a proper p. He describes a closure condition for the full subcategory of admissible coefficients in terms of a diagram indexed by a category with a final object, restriction away from that object, and the value at the final object. The reservation “sauf erreur” qualifies this discussion.

**077 · Proposition 4 and its corollary.** For a proper functor he gives a comparison between slices along each base arrow. The dual criterion involves a smooth functor and the opposite kind of slice. The small corollary beside the paragraph specializes to a Cat-cofibrant functor and mentions the dual Cat-fibration case. The name “parfaites” is suggested for certain fibrations that are both proper and smooth.

**077–078 · Proposition 5 and Definition 3.** The two conditions are that f remain a D-equivalence under every base change and that f be a D-fibration with D-contractible fibres. The text refers to proposition 2 for the fibration part and to proposition 3(b) for the converse. The next sheet considers what these conditions would imply for adjunction on diagram categories. Definition 3 gives the name “triviale”.

**G6a · 078–079, proposition 6(a).** For a triangle over S, a subset meeting each connected component can be used to test a sufficient fibre condition. The proof shows how the equivalence property propagates along an arrow of S. One square compares a fibre with a slice; the other compares slices at opposite ends of a base arrow. Grothendieck describes the set of good objects as a union of connected components.

**078 and 080 · Proposition 6(b).** The asserted necessity is annotated “Faux” and “Pas prouvé !”. The attempted proof on 080 compares two locally constant direct images and is struck through. The live text asks what follows even for a product projection and writes a test involving the maps α with index M.

**G81m · 081, counterexample and monodromy.** Grothendieck first works with topological spaces. The base and fibre are circles and the monodromy reverses orientation. He asserts an integral homology comparison modulo 2-torsion and an isomorphism with coefficients in a ring in which 2 is invertible, although the circle fibre is not contractible for the indicated nonzero coefficients. The upper-right margin suggests a two-sheeted covering. The lower half starts an ordered-set realization, with a four-vertex diagram for the base and restrictions of X to its pieces and their intersection.

## Remarks of the model

- **R1 · Model remark, high confidence · 078–079.** The propagation lemma addresses the sufficient condition in 6(a). A subset whose membership is unchanged across each arrow is a union of components of the underlying undirected graph. This step alone gives no converse for a map already known to be an equivalence.
- **R2 · Model remark, high confidence · 081.** The choice of the number 2 agrees with the stated monodromy. On the degree-one generator of a circle the orientation reversal acts by −1, so subtracting that action from the identity gives multiplication by 2. This checks the coefficient factor in the example. The integral homology assertion above is recorded as Grothendieck’s.
- **R3 · Model remark, medium confidence · 070, 073–074 and 080.** The notes return to the distinction between a constant coefficient and a locally constant one. The clearest textual link is the question on 074; the attempted argument on 080 likewise stops upon introducing the case in which both coefficients are constant. The cancelled writing leaves open how far the author meant to identify the two arguments.
