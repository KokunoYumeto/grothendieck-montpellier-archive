# Quadratic torsors and λ-split extensions

Cote 83, scans 162–177. Scan references below are the PDF scan numbers. The French in quotation marks comes from these sheets. A “possible correction” concerns the argument only; the corresponding reading and its uncertainty are retained in the diplomatic text.

## What the pages do

**162–163 · From a quadratic divisor to a quadratic algebra.** The quadratic function has prescribed homogeneous part on the affine line. After twisting the form to H, Grothendieck reads the determinant and unit of a quadratic algebra from it. Its polar form at the unit gives the trace. He then compares an involution of the ambient affine object with the canonical involution of the divisor. Taking the identity involution in characteristic two gives his objection to mere stability of the divisor.

**164–165 · Choosing the sign of the parameter.** A point ζ of the product torsor and its compatible quadratic function are related by ζ + qζ(x) = x*x. Translating ζ by α translates the function in the opposite direction. The fibre description of the divisor is kept alongside this calculation. In coordinates an origin changes the affine involution to x ↦ τ−x.

**166–168 · The universal target Q.** Adding constant functions makes the set Λ of the prescribed functions an L tensor-square torsor. The evaluation pairing on Λ×A produces a map A → Qσ, with Qσ described using maps of torsors from Λ to the tensor square. Choosing an origin identifies the target with that square. The marked formula on 168 records the effect of translating the argument and identifies the divisor as the inverse image of a section. The two terms involving α have different signs.

**169–170 · Pairing quadratic forms.** For two affine lines with involution he forms A₁*A₂. A putative product description of its Q immediately raises the question of an involution on each target. He can nevertheless restrict the tensor of two forms to construct a map from Q(A₁)×Q(A₂). In coordinates its constant coefficient is b₂²c₁+b₁²c₂−4c₁c₂. Translating one input adds its increment times the discriminant of the other. The discriminant of the result is the product of the two discriminants.

**170–171 · Comparing the two routes through the diagram.** The upper row takes the product of points, the middle row the product of quadratic forms, and the bottom row multiplies their values in the tensor-square lines. Grothendieck expands the form belonging to a product of points and the form obtained by first applying the two projections. Choosing the points themselves as origins is his proposed shortcut. He then explains the obstruction to writing the target as a star product using his present definition.

**172–173 · A scalar section of an extension.** For an involution on E inducing minus the identity on the kernel and the identity on the quotient, x+σ(x) factors through F. This gives π from F to E with επ equal to twice the identity. He then replaces 2 by λ. He interprets the λ-section as a splitting of the scalar-transformed extension, and, when F=k, as an element t with ε(t)=λ, giving an origin on the associated torsor.

**174–175 · The complementary map.** With an origin t, the affine map to the translation module is x ↦ λx−t. For λ=2 this gives the negative of σ(x)−x. On the whole module he uses u(x)=λx−πε(x); its values lie in the kernel and its restriction there is scalar multiplication. Conversely λx−u(x) factors through F, recovering π.

**176 · The multilinear universal problem.** For extensions with the same scalar λ, Grothendieck specifies a pair of multilinear maps f and f⁰, the latter on kernel factors. Moving one argument of f by an element of its kernel contributes a value of f⁰ formed with the u-maps on the other arguments. On kernel arguments he writes f = λⁿ⁻¹ f⁰. He names the representing module by the indexed star.

**176–177 · Quotient, exact sequence and splittings.** He describes the representing module as a quotient of the direct sum of the tensor of the Eᵢ and the tensor of their kernels. Killing the f⁰ component leaves maps through the Fᵢ. He distinguishes the resulting exact sequence from any claim of injectivity on the left. With chosen splittings he writes Eᵢ as a direct sum and ends this excerpt with the components from Fᵢ to Eᵢ⁰.

## Remarks of the model

- **M170 · Model remark, high confidence.** As polynomial identities, the two translation laws and the factorization of the discriminant follow from the constant C written at the top of 170. I checked the three residual polynomials independently by expansion (C170a–c). This does not determine the branch on the handwritten q subscript.
- **M171 · Model remark, high confidence about the polynomial check.** With s = x₁b₂+b₁x₂−2x₁x₂ and cᵢ = xᵢ(bᵢ−xᵢ), the coordinate constant C equals s(b₁b₂−s). The first-input polynomial also factors as displayed. Both differences expanded to zero (C171a–b). This check leaves the doubtful ink at S171b open.
- **M172 · Model remark, high confidence.** The factorization used on 172 explains involutivity: writing T=πε gives T²=2T when επ=2 id. Hence (T−id)²=id. This short composition check uses the boxed scalar relation and the preceding expression for σ.
- **M175 · Model remark, high confidence.** In u=λ id−πε, composing with ε gives zero by επ=λ id. Restricting to ker ε gives λ id. These are exactly the two conditions needed for λ id−u to descend to F. The argument uses the unobscured right sides of the equations, independently of the overwritten name of the map.
- **M177 · Model remark, high confidence.** The sequence as written starts at the tensor of the kernels, with no zero before it. Exactness there supplies no assertion of left injectivity. This accounts for the separate question and splitting argument on the same sheet.

**Reading question, scan 177, L19.** The frozen reading has a doubtful cancelled t under the live b in bᵢ. A possible reverse layering would give a t over a b. The thick loop and cross-stroke obscure which was laid down last. This changes the name of the component map; its shown source and target are clear.
