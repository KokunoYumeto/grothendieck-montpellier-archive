# Mathematical notes · K157-1-14

## What the pages do

**U1 · Pullback tests for classes of arrows, 212.** Formula (4.10) relates the aspherical and smooth classes by testing base changes. The second test says what the base-change functor does to arrows of the chosen class. In (4.11.) the author starts with a class A and collects the base changes preserving it. In (4.12) he starts with a class L of base changes and tests an arrow against W after every such change.

**U2 · Two closures, 212–213.** The author compares stability under isomorphism, composition and change of base. He composes the two constructions in both orders, obtaining the inclusions (4.13) and (4.14). He denotes the resulting operations on A and L by a raised sharp and flat, respectively, and records their idempotence. The lower indices of the operators on 212 are partly cancelled.

**U3 · Four arrow-class examples, 213.** The first starts with Et and ends with Liss; the second starts with the class written Coet./Coét. and ends with Prop. The third starts with A = W and gives Fib with subscript W and A-sharp = W. The fourth takes all arrows of Cat, gives the class with subscript univ, and has L-flat = L.

**U4 · A relation on ordered sets, 214–215.** Section 5 begins with a relation R between P and Q. The polar of a set consists of all elements related to each of its members. Condition (D) requires each polar to be open and to have a greatest element, written β(A) or α(B). With arbitrary suprema available, the author describes the corresponding conditions on the fibres over single elements. Formula (5.2.) sends unions in either variable to intersections. He uses this and the description of intersections of principal open parts to obtain the infima in (5.3.).

**U5 · Double polars and the role of order, 215–217.** Composing the two polar operations gives A-sharp and B-flat. The original set is contained in its double polar, a polar is already closed, and closure is idempotent. The author interrupts the ordered argument to distinguish facts valid for any relation. He gives the polars and closures of the empty sets and identifies each closure as the smallest closed part containing the given set.

**U6 · Closed parts and their correspondence, 217–218.** Proposition 5.1 gives inverse decreasing bijections between the closed parts on the two sides. Corollaire 5.2 states that an intersection of closed parts is closed. Its displayed polar is the closure on the other side of the union of their polars. The annotation below that expression calls it a supremum of closed parts.

**U7 · The relation on powersets, 218–219.** Formula (5.13) replaces the two sets by their powersets. A pair of parts satisfies the new relation precisely when their product is contained in R. Fixing one part gives all parts contained in its polar, a principal open part on the other side. The author returns to ordered sets because the objects or their relation need not have arisen from this powerset construction.

**U8 · Recovering the relation from point maps, 219–220.** Sending a point to its principal open part is injective. Formula (5.15) puts β₀ on P and α₀ on Q. They are the values of the greatest-element maps on singletons. In (5.17) membership of (p,q) in R is equivalent both to q ≤ β₀(p) and to p ≤ α₀(q). Formula (5.18) expresses each point map as the supremum of a fibre determined by the other.

**U9 · A test for a dualizing relation, 220–222.** The author asks which point maps occur. Decrease of β₀ makes the relevant q-fibres open. Condition (b) says that β₀ sends arbitrary suprema in P to infima in Q. The author interprets this in terms of a functor out of the oppositely ordered P and a left adjoint in the reverse direction.

**U10 · Closed elements, 222–223.** Formula (5.19) defines point closures by the two composites. Formula (5.20) compares a point closure with the greatest element of the closure of its singleton. The text describes the closed elements as fixed points, and p-sharp as the least closed element above p. Formula (5.22.) gives the corresponding inverse maps on closed elements.

**U11 · Orthogonality, 223–224.** Example 1 uses a pairing of modules. The small addition treats finite dimension and the sideways note describes weakly closed vector subspaces in what appears to be the infinite case. Example 2 passes to dual topological groups and identifies closed parts as closed subgroups.

**U12 · Polarity and semi-polarity, 224.** Example 3 uses an absolute-value bound on a pairing of vector spaces. In the real and complex cases the closed parts are described as weakly closed convex sets with a further qualifier read as “cerclées”. Example 4 gives a one-sided bound and describes the weakly closed convex sets containing the origin. The comparison sign before −1 is overwritten.

**U13 · Galois example, 224.** In example 5 the relation between an element of L and an element σ of its Galois group is the fixed-point condition σ.x = x. The page identifies the two kinds of closed parts as subextensions and subgroups. It mentions analogues in inseparable cases and descent statements.

**U14 · Quillen’s example, 225–226.** The new objects are classes of arrows of a category M. The author expresses their relation using inclusions involving a lower star on Φ and an upper star on Ψ. He names the left and right lifting properties, then distinguishes the set D of commutative squares from the part D₀ admitting a diagonal that makes both triangles commute. He describes D₀ as an image involving Fl₃(M), gives the map from the square of the 1-simplex to the 3-simplex on its four vertices, and displays the projection to the two boundary arrows. The packet ends just as he announces the condition on this projection and D₀.

## Remarks of the model

- **M1 · High.** The distinction the author makes on 216 can be located in the types of the formulas. Interchanging unions and intersections of polars in (5.2.) refers only to sets and a relation. The statement about infima in (5.3.) also names greatest elements in the two orders. The exact digit in the back-reference remains doubtful.
- **M2 · Low as a slip.** The second row of (5.20) places A-sharp on the left and a supremum of point closures on the right. Its first row explicitly passes from a closed subset to its greatest element. The text does not specify here whether a similar identification or a supremum among closed elements is understood in the second row. The later assertion about commuting with arbitrary suprema is crossed out. I would leave this as a question about interpretation.
- **M3 · Low.** On 225 the parenthesis explaining LLP and RLP is hard to reconcile with both preceding inclusions if every class letter is read literally. The first class glyph is overwritten, with a possible extra mark. That prevents a secure correction or attribution of an error.
- **M4 · High.** On 226 the square includes its two horizontal arrows. Its image under the displayed projection retains the vertical arrows i and p. Membership in D₀ records the existence of a diagonal making the two triangles commute. The final quantifier or condition is not visible in the supplied material.
- **RQ1 · Reading question, low confidence.** On scan 213, the line beginning “On a, pour une” above (4.13) has a plain W between the inclusion signs in the frozen transcription. Its right upright is darker and wider than the W added to (4.13). The ink allows either a retraced plain W or a doubled or overwritten W. The two shapes alone do not establish a different alphabet.
