# Mathematical notes on scans 197–211

## What the pages do

**197–200 · End of the argument for 4.1.** Grothendieck calls the common preliminary condition \(A\). He invokes duality and cofinality, then evaluates the counit \(u_!u^*i\to i\) at \(j\). Over an arrow \(j\to i\) he identifies the fibre by the connected components of a two-sided slice. After announcing the end of the proof, he reopens the argument for \(c'\). For the unit, testing maps into representables leaves the question whether \(u_{j!}e_{I'_{/j}}\to j\) is an isomorphism.

**200 · Corollary 4.2.** Universal \(W_0\)-equivalence suffices for \(W_0\)-connexity. He states the criterion using the fibres and the pullbacks over nonidentity arrows of \(I\).

**200–202 · Examples in 4.3.** An equivalence can miss an object and have an empty fibre. Even for ordered sets, the four-object example over \(\Delta^2\) has connected inverse images over all six displayed intervals, while pullback over the edge \(0\to2\) is disconnected. He uses that same edge to rule out his proposed factorization into an equivalence followed by a universal \(W_0\)-equivalence.

**202–203 · Corollary 4.3.** For a \(W_0\)-smooth map, a \(W_0\)-proper map, or a weak \(W_0\)-fibration, connexity and universal equivalence agree. The proof compares a fibre with a slice. He then tests a different proposed characterization by localization. A connected groupoid which is not 1-connected, mapped to the point, supplies his counterexample.

**204–207 · Proposition 4.4.** For a derivator with the indicated pointwise adjoints, the three slice conditions \(b,b',b''\) imply the three equivalent conditions \(a,a',a''\) expressing full faithfulness of \(u^*\). The converse holds in four listed cases. These concern a preordered base, essentially surjective restrictions \(\alpha_i^*\), conservative functors \(\alpha_{i!}\), and the set-valued case.

**205–207 · Proof of 4.4.** The fibre calculation first tests coefficients \(F_i=\alpha_i^*F\) coming from the base. The local condition tests every coefficient on the slice. Adjunction rewrites the restricted tests as an isomorphism after applying \(\alpha_{i!}\). Conservativity lets him recover the isomorphism before that application.

**207–208 · Example 4.5.** For the opposite of the set-valued example, Grothendieck separates the conclusion of 4.4 from its conservativity hypothesis. He uses two parallel arrows \(0\rightrightarrows1\) and calculates \(\beta_{i*}\) by a product. Setting \(G'_1=\varnothing\) leaves the diagram \(\varnothing\rightrightarrows G_0\), which loses the \(G_1\) component.

**209–210 · Definition and Corollary 4.7.** The heading read as 4.6 names the \(b\)-conditions \(\mathbb D\)-biasphericity and the \(a\)-conditions \(\mathbb D\)-connexity. If binary coproduct in \(A=\mathbb D(e)\) is conservative, respectively faithful, then \(u_!\) has the same property for étale \(u\). He calculates it on discrete fibres. The coproduct hypothesis or its product dual gives equivalence of connexity and biasphericity. The oblique note on 209 singles out nonempty Hom sets and mentions a zero object and additive categories.

**210–211 · Open case and Corollary 4.8.** After recording the example he does not know, Grothendieck shows where universal equivalence sits among seven classes of maps. It implies biasphericity and hence connexity. For a smooth map, a proper map or a weak fibration, biasphericity gives the converse. Below the diagram he recalls unit and counit tests using constant coefficients for asphericity and coasphericity, then arbitrary coefficients for connexity. The last sentence continues beyond scan 211.

- **207.** The parenthetic NB says that \(\alpha_{i!}\) “est tjrs conservatif” in the presheaf case. He uses this to recover case \(4^\circ\) from \(3^\circ\).

## Remarks of the model

- **R201 · My remark, high confidence.** I counted components in the four-object diagram on 201. In the manuscript's order the six inverse images have \(1,1,1,1,1,1\) components. Restricting to the edge with endpoints 0 and 2 gives two. The distinction depends on retaining the middle vertex in the interval \([0,2]\).
- **R208 · My remark, high confidence.** Take \(G_0\) to be a singleton and \(G'_1\) empty. The two functions from a singleton \(G_1\) to a two-element \(G_1\) give distinct maps of forks. Both have the same image, the identity of \(\varnothing\rightrightarrows G_0\). This supplies finite witnesses for both failures asserted on 208.
- **R209 · My remark, high confidence for the finite check.** The underlined “non vides” on 209 matters precisely where the example on 208 used an empty factor. I enumerated all 3,136 pairs of functions between nonempty labelled sets of sizes 1 to 3. In all 81 cases where the product was bijective, both factors were bijective.
