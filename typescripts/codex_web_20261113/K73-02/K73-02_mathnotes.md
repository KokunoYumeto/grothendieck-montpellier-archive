Starting with $u_0,u_1$ as a basis, the writer specifies $u_2$, solves for $u_3$ and begins expressions for later sides. Several of those expressions stop on this sheet.

**Scans 22–24.** Three polynomial coefficients $P_i,Q_i,R_i$ express later vectors in $u_0,u_1,u_2$. The writer gives recurrences and initial entries, then writes a closure relation numbered (0). The identifications $u_n=u_0$, $u_{n+1}=u_1$, $u_{n+2}=u_2$ supply (1)–(3). The four rows are gathered into a system with coefficients $A_j^n,B_j^n,C_j^n$. Scan 24 gives the polynomial rings for these coefficients.

**Scans 24–26.** A linear relation on $u_0,u_1+u_2,u_3$ determines a one-dimensional space of coefficient triples when the three vectors span the plane. The writer asks when every such triple is collinear. With $u_0,u_1$ independent, he follows the successive directions using $\alpha_i$, then $\beta_i$. The calculation leads to three-periodic sides and $u_0+u_1+u_2=0$.

**Scans 26–28.** The proposition defines $N(u_*)$, the space of scalar sequences satisfying the cyclic vector relations. It compares a dimension condition and the existence of a sequence with all coefficients nonzero to conditions on the sides. The proof maps a sequence to three consecutive coefficients, bounds the dimension using the rank of a triple of vectors, and on scan 28 tests three-periodic coefficients.

**Scan 29.** A theorem arranges the possibilities for $N(u_*)$ by dimension and by containment in coordinate hyperplanes. The marginal names distinguish the general case, two special cases and a hyperspecial case. The last paragraph relates zero coefficients to repeated vertices and proposes a criterion and a normalization when $\lambda_0=0$.

## Remarks of the model

- **R18 · Medium confidence, scan 18.** I interpret the juxtaposition between the two parenthesized sums under the question mark as an exterior product. On substituting the displayed value of $u_3$, both differences between the three expressions vanish identically in two coordinates. The expansion under the brace supports the interpretation.
- **R26 · Low confidence about the scope of the proposition, scans 24, 26 and 28.** Over $\mathbb Q$, take $u_0,u_1,u_2,u_3$ to be $1,2,3,-6$ times the same nonzero vector. This is a closed sequence. All the triples in b are collinear, yet $u_3\ne u_0$ and the first three sides sum to six times that vector. Thus b does not imply d for arbitrary nonzero sides in a plane. The margin on scan 24 assumes $u_0,u_1$ independent, and the corresponding part of the statement on scan 26 is heavily cancelled. Requiring the sides to span the plane would exclude this example. The full equivalence with that hypothesis has not been checked.
- **R28 · Low confidence about the scope of the field assumption, scans 26–29.** Over $\mathbb F_2$, the cyclic triangle $(1,0),(0,1),(1,1)$ satisfies d. The four solutions of the relation on scan 28 are $000,011,101,110$, so none has all three coefficients nonzero as a requires. Scan 27 explicitly uses « $k$ étant infini ». The example explains why carrying that qualification into the last implication matters.
- **RQ21 · Reading question, low confidence, scan 21, lines 19–21.** The frozen vertex labels use $s$. On the four inner corners the sign has a tall stem and a small closed loop at the bottom. A handwritten $\delta$ is a plausible reading there, especially at the two upper corners. The outer edge labels have different strokes.
- **RQ24 · Reading question, low confidence, scan 24, line 25.** The frozen reading is $u_1^3$ in $u_1+u_2=u_1^3$. A possible reading is $u'_1$. The raised stroke curls at its foot like a small 3, whereas a prime would make the right side a new name for the sum. The transcription retains the frozen exponent.
