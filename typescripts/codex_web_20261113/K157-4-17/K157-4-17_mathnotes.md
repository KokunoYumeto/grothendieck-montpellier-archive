# Mathematical notes · K157-4-17

## What the pages do

**Scans 258–259, end of an argument and Lemma 9.10.** The opening fragment compares a map out of a coproduct with its effect on connected components. The lemma considers a map inverted in $\mathrm{Hot}_W$ whose map on $\pi_0$ fails to be surjective. It gives a collapse to the point category for the indicated subcategory. The two alternatives for $\mathrm{Hot}_W$ are $e$ and $\Delta^1$, distinguished by the map from the empty category to $e$. The short argument on 259 recalls a homotopy using two points in different components.

**Scan 259, Proposition 9.11.** Three conditions are presented as equivalent. The first two involve Loc(5 bis), connectivity, and the exclusion of the two small possibilities for $\mathrm{Hot}_W$. The third tests membership of a functor in $W$ on $\pi_0$ and on the induced component maps. The paragraph after the proposition explains why the author does not give the last property a separate Loc number.

**Scans 261–262, Proposition 9.12.** The proposition relates the connectivity axiom to a test on parallel arrows, a nonconstant Hom map, two conditions on the localised categories and the number of isomorphism classes. The proof factors $\pi_0$ through $\mathrm{Hot}_W$. Its small diagram organizes the implications already obtained. The remaining implication is stated as Corollary 9.13.

**Scans 262–264, Corollary 9.13.** Failure of connectivity makes the Hom map constant. A quotient category written $\mathscr H$ distinguishes empty and nonempty categories. Inverting the arrows out of the empty category produces $\mathscr H'$. The proof treats a failure of injectivity on components and then a failure of surjectivity. In the latter case it uses two functors into a discrete two-object category, two sections, and the two copies of $X$ in $X\amalg X$ to conclude that the original parallel arrows become equal.

**Scans 260, 265–267, Theorem 10.1.** The theorem collects consequences for $\mathrm{HOT}_W$. Part 1 treats adjoints, their calculation, cartesian and cocartesian squares and the long sequences of suspension and cosuspension. Part 2 treats decomposition, localisation and strong saturation. Part 3 compares pullback on constant objects and on the full subcategories carrying the superscript lc, including for induced derivators. The closing comment distinguishes earlier constructions from the ingredients reserved for the proof.

**Scan 268, chapter XVI contents.** Two lists record the proposed Loc axioms and their grouping into sections. The cover moves from saturation and final objects to fibrations, limits, accessibility and descent. A comment by Loc 10 connects a proposed group of axioms with $\mathcal W_\infty$. The lower list also reserves a section for relations with derivator axioms.

**Scans 269–270, foundational axioms and Definition 1.1.** Loc 1 and its variants distinguish several saturation conditions. Loc 2 uses a final object, with a weaker segment condition alongside it. Loc 3 uses the categories localised above the objects of $S$. A reminder passes to opposite categories and introduces corresponding dual axioms. Definition 1.1 calls a class of arrows in $\mathrm{Fl}(\mathrm{Cat})$ satisfying Loc(1,2,3) a *localiseur fondamental*.

**Scan 271, Rappel 1.2 and the strong localisation axiom.** The reminder concerns homotopisms and parallel homotopic functors. Loc(3 bis) considers a morphism over $S$ and the induced morphism between the categories localised over each $s$. The text identifies Loc(3) as the case $Y=S$.

**Scan 271, Proposition 1.3.** Two lines compare systems of axioms using Loc(2) on the left and a lowered zero after 2 on the right. The right-hand third axiom is primed. A canceled formulation below starts with two split fibred categories and a strict cartesian functor. Its last clause ends at “le foncteur”.

**Scan 272, canceled typescript P 31.** The author describes Serre’s role in his encounter with sheaves, then names Zariski, Weil, Leray and Serre as immediate predecessors. Section 11 turns to schemes and toposes. Its footnote situates the beginnings of the development in 1958 and associates the more intrinsic notion of topos, especially from 1963, with étale cohomology.
